• A feed pays out rarely and at random. Dopamine neurons respond to rewards that are better than expected, and an unpredictable payout keeps that signal coming.
  • Repeated thousands of times, checking becomes a habit that runs on cues rather than on enjoyment.
  • Bad news gets a head start: threats grab attention, angry and out-group posts spread further, and a feed that never ends removes the natural place to stop.
  • At night the main cost is lost sleep time; screen light adds a smaller push on the body clock.
  • Measured effects on wellbeing are small, and "dopamine addiction" is not established. Design changes that give the feed an end shorten sessions.

It starts with one look. Twenty minutes later you are still going, reading about a disaster in a country you cannot place. You don't feel better. You keep going anyway. Since around 2020 this has been called doomscrolling, and researchers treat it as a distinct habit, more than interest in the news . This page builds up the brain's reward and habit systems with models you can run, then asks why bad news holds attention, what scrolling does to sleep, and which claims survive the studies.

The feed

Notice the gaps between the orange stems: two arrive back to back, then forty posts pass with nothing. Each hit gives a big positive surprise and lifts the expectation; through the filler it sags, and the next hit lifts it again. If good posts came like clockwork, you could stop right after one. At random, the next could always be one flick away. That surprise signal is, almost exactly, what a group of neurons computes.

Prediction error

In the 1980s and 1990s Wolfram Schultz and colleagues recorded single dopamine neurons in monkeys learning that a light meant juice a second or two later . Each row below is one trial, each tick one spike; I've rebuilt the recording with a small model. Raise the number of training trials and watch where the burst goes:

Untrained, the neuron ticks along at a few spikes a second, then bursts at the juice. With that burst fades and a new burst appears at the light, though the juice is as sweet as ever. from a trained animal and the neuron goes quiet, a dip at the moment the juice was due.

So the neuron signals the difference between what happened and what was expected: better, a burst; as expected, nothing; worse, a pause. This is a reward prediction error. After training the light gets the burst because the light is now the surprise: it comes unannounced and says juice is on the way.

Dopamine neurons respond to better than expected, not to good. A reward you fully expect produces almost no response; a cue that signals an unexpected reward produces a big one.

The pattern matched a learning rule from computer science, temporal-difference learning . It keeps an estimate V of the reward to expect from each moment of the trial and, at every step, computes an error:

\green{\delta} = \orange{r} + \gamma\, V(\text{next}) - V(\text{now})

Here \orange{r} is the reward that just arrived and \gamma, slightly below 1, makes later rewards count a little less. Each estimate moves a fraction \alpha, the learning rate, toward what the next moment says. Here is the model behind the raster: learned value on top, error below. Step through the trials, then try a and a :

On the first trial the only error is at the juice, \green{\delta = 1}, because nothing predicted it. The model raises its estimate for the moment just before, so next time the error appears one step earlier, and value fills in backward until only the light is a surprise. Recordings often look as if the burst jumps straight to the cue, but a 2022 study in mice found the gradual backward shift the model predicts .

What the model leaves out 2 min

The model here represents each 0.2-second moment after the light as its own state and has a single reward. Real dopamine neurons also respond to novelty, to some aversive events, and to how vigorous a movement will be, and different groups of them project to different targets with somewhat different signals. Timing is also uncertain for the animal: a cue that predicts juice "in about two seconds" leaves the expectation smeared out in time, which is part of why a reward that comes early still produces a small burst. None of this changes the core result. The prediction-error account explains most of the classic recordings with one equation, which is why it has lasted.

These neurons sit in two small clusters in the midbrain, under the cerebral hemispheres. Here is the brain at real size, its cortex see-through. Turn it, pick a pathway, and point at a structure, or its name here. The ventral tegmental area sends dopamine to the nucleus accumbens at the base of the striatum, the amygdala, and the prefrontal cortex; the substantia nigra sends it to the upper striatum, the caudate and putamen :

The orange fibers are what people mean by "the reward system". The striatum is where actions get linked to the cues that precede them, which is why the same circuit turns up in learning, motivation, and habit. And the neurons fire for any better-than-expected outcome: food, money, a funny clip, a message from a friend.

"Dopamine is the pleasure chemical." Rats whose dopamine is almost completely depleted still show the facial reactions of liking a sweet taste; what they lose is the drive to work for it . Dopamine is better described as a learning and wanting signal than as the feeling of enjoyment.

Unpredictable rewards

Long before anyone recorded a dopamine neuron, Charles Ferster and B. F. Skinner measured how the timing of rewards shapes behavior . An animal pressed a lever and a pen climbed one notch per press, so the slope of the line was the pressing rate. A fixed ratio pays every nth press; a variable ratio pays every n presses on average, so any press might pay. A fixed interval pays the first press after a set time; a variable interval after an unpredictable time. A simulated animal, following rules that summarize real animals, runs all four. Set the schedule to presses (and twice that many seconds) and watch the variable ratio line:

The variable ratio line is the steepest and smoothest. On a fixed ratio the animal pauses after each reward, since the next is a known distance away; on a fixed interval it speeds up as the reward comes due, drawing scallops. On a variable ratio there is never a good moment to stop. That is the schedule of a slot machine and, loosely, of a feed.

"Loosely" matters: a feed's content is chosen by an algorithm, its rewards differ in size and kind, and nobody has measured scrolling with Skinner's precision. The comparison describes the payout's structure, not a proven mechanism. The dopamine results do suggest why unpredictability matters. First, a question:

A monkey learns several cues, each followed by juice with a different probability: never, one time in four, half the time, three in four, always. Between the cue and the moment the juice may come, at which probability do its dopamine neurons ramp up the most?

The quick burst at the cue grows with the probability, but a slower ramp of activity between cue and outcome peaks when the outcome is least certain, at 50%.

Unpredictable rewards also make behavior hard to switch off. In 1939 Lloyd Humphreys found that people trained to blink to a light paired with a puff of air kept blinking longer after the puffs stopped if they had been puffed on only half the trials . This partial reinforcement extinction effect appears across many species and tasks. Here is one account of it as a model: the animal presses while it still believes the old rules hold, and each unpaid press lowers that belief by an amount that depends on how often presses used to pay. From goal to habit

People often keep scrolling after it stops being fun. Two ways of running an action explain that. A goal-directed action is done for its result: you open the app to see your friend's post. A habit is triggered by the situation: in bed, phone in hand, the thumb moves.

Researchers tell them apart with outcome devaluation. Train a rat to press a lever for food, make the food unappealing (let it eat its fill, or pair the food with mild nausea), then offer the lever with no food delivered. A goal-directed rat barely presses; a habitual one presses anyway . In this model a goal-directed controller checks what the food is worth now and a habit controller only remembers how often pressing paid. Give the rat more training:

After the devalued rat presses about a tenth as much. After it presses nearly as much as a rat whose food is still wanted: the behavior has come loose from its outcome. In rats, damage to the dorsomedial striatum makes behavior habitual early, and damage to the dorsolateral striatum keeps it goal-directed after long training , so habit formation looks like control shifting from the first to the second.

In people it is less tidy. One imaging study saw the human dorsolateral region grow more active with overtraining , but a later paper reported five failed attempts to make volunteers habitual by overtraining in the lab . Everyday habits clearly exist; making them on cue has proved hard.

How long do habits take? Phillippa Lally and colleagues had 96 volunteers pick a new daily behavior, like a glass of water after breakfast, and rate each day how automatic it felt . Each person's ratings were fitted with a curve that rises and levels off. Here are 39 such curves, spread like the people whose data fitted well. Move the day to :

The median was 66 days to reach 95% of the plateau, with a range of 18 to 254; anything past day 84, when the study ended, is extrapolated. The curves rise fastest early, and missing one day did not set people back. Checking a phone gets dozens of repetitions a day.

A habit is behavior that keeps going after its reward has lost its value. That is why "I don't even enjoy this" and "I can't stop" can both be true.

Why bad news

None of this explains why the scrolling so often goes to bad news. Four more pieces help: where a stopping point would come from, what grabs attention, what spreads, and what checking does to worry.

Start with stopping. A bird feeding on a berry bush gets less the longer it stays, so its gain flattens, and moving to the next bush costs travel time. Eric Charnov's marginal value theorem says to leave where a line from the start of the journey just touches the gain curve . On a feed, travel to the next post is a flick of the thumb, and the : an algorithm keeps supplying posts, so the gain never flattens and the touching point that says "this patch is used up" never comes. Applying foraging theory to feeds is an analogy, but it names something real: a printed newspaper ends, a feed does not.

Next, attention. Threatening things tend to be noticed faster than neutral ones. Arne Öhman and colleagues had people find the odd picture in a grid: a snake or spider among flowers was found faster than a flower among snakes, and finding the threat barely slowed as the grid grew . Here is that pattern with illustrative numbers. Enlarge the grid and compare the threat times with the neutral ones:

At the two are close; at the neutral search is about half a second slower and the threat search has barely moved. Danger-flagging systems, including the amygdala, are thought to steer attention before you have looked at everything. The size of the advantage is debated, and some may come from simple features like a snake's curves. The broader negativity bias is well replicated: across 17 countries, skin conductance and heart-rate variability responded more to negative news clips than to positive ones .

Then, what spreads. In half a million tweets about gun control, same-sex marriage, and climate change, each moral-emotional word ("outrage", "shameful") raised retweets by about 20% . In 2.7 million posts from news outlets and members of Congress, each word about the political out-group raised the odds of a share by about 67% . Build a post and watch the expected shares:

Words about the other side do the most: with a post is expected to be shared more than four times as often as a neutral one. Negative and moral-emotional words add less, and positive words slightly reduce sharing. These are correlations, but they held across eight datasets. In 105,000 headline variants tested at random on Upworthy, each extra negative word raised clicks by about 2.3% . Feeds rank posts partly by engagement, so these effects shape what you see.

Last, worry. After the 2013 Boston Marathon bombings, people who followed coverage six or more hours a day reported more acute stress than people who had been at the scene . Following that sample for three years showed a cycle: exposure predicted distress, distress predicted worry about the future, and worry predicted heavier exposure to the next disaster . Checking is partly an attempt to resolve uncertainty, and dopamine neurons respond to advance information about a reward much as to the reward itself . Self-control

At six in the evening most people would pick a good night's sleep over another hour on the phone; at eleven, many pick the phone. If the value of a reward fell by the same fraction for every hour of delay (exponential discounting), preferences between two fixed options would never flip. People and animals instead discount hyperbolically, steeply at short delays and gently at long ones . Here are an hour of scrolling at 11 pm and feeling rested at 7 am, as seen at :

At the rested morning wins. As 11 pm nears, the scrolling hour's value shoots up and overtakes it around 10 pm, though neither option changed. With the curves never cross. The numbers are made up, but the reversal is robust, and it fits bedtime procrastination, going to bed later than intended for no outside reason, which one survey linked to too little sleep .

Quick two-way choices are modeled as noisy evidence drifting toward one of two thresholds, a drift-diffusion model . Treat each post as one: put the phone down or next post. The goal pushes evidence up, the urge pulls it down, and if nothing is decided in a second and a half the thumb flicks, because swiping is the default. Set the urge and the goal:

The chance of stopping on any one post is small, and a small per-post chance means a long session: 3% per post means about 33 posts. Raise the goal a little and it climbs fast. Two features favor scrolling regardless of willpower: the default is to keep going, and every post is a fresh decision, so an urge that wins only sometimes still wins the session.

The older explanation was ego depletion: willpower as a fuel that runs down, leaving you unable to resist the phone after a long day. A 2010 meta-analysis of 83 studies found a medium-sized effect . Before you reveal what came next:

The original literature was many small studies, and small studies that happen to find big effects are the ones that get published. So "you scroll at night because your willpower is used up" is not well supported. Discounting and defaults are better candidates, and both suggest changing the situation rather than trying harder.

Attention

Scrolling rarely happens alone; it interrupts something else. What does a switch actually cost? The cleanest measurements come from the lab. In Robert Rogers and Stephen Monsell's version, people see a letter and a digit and classify one of them, and the task alternates in a fixed rhythm: two trials on letters, two on digits, and so on . With little time to prepare, a switch costs over a fifth of a second. With more than about half a second it drops, then stops at about 115 milliseconds no matter how long you wait . That leftover, the residual switch cost, suggests part of the reconfiguration can only happen once the next task is actually in front of you . But note the scale: tenths of a second. If that were the whole cost, switching would be nearly free. Now add up an hour of them, and also count the time that a check pulls you away into something else. Twenty switches an hour cost about four seconds of measurable switch time. One minute of detour after each costs twenty minutes. The expensive part of an interruption is not the switch; it is what you switch to, and whether you come back. In a field study of 24 office workers, Gloria Mark and colleagues found that 57% of work episodes were interrupted, and interrupted work, when resumed the same day, was picked up again about 25 minutes later on average, usually after two other activities .

"It takes 23 minutes to refocus after a distraction." The number comes from Mark's research on how long people took to return to an interrupted task, with other work in between, not from a measurement of lost concentration. Lab switch costs are measured in tenths of a second.

Sleep

Much doomscrolling happens in bed, in the dark, with a bright rectangle a hand's length away. Light at night suppresses melatonin, the hormone released in the evening as a darkness signal, and shifts the body clock later, mostly through melanopsin, a blue-sensitive pigment in a few retinal cells. So evening light is measured as melanopic EDI, lux weighted by how strongly they drive melanopsin.

What the evidence shows

How much do these mechanisms affect people? The most-cited evidence is surveys correlating screen time with wellbeing. Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski noticed that one survey can be analyzed thousands of defensible ways, with results depending on the choice. So they ran them all, a specification curve analysis, on three datasets covering over 355,000 adolescents, alongside other factors . Pick a factor to see its association in 400 simulated teenagers:

The technology bar is negative, but across all the analyses it explains at most about 0.4% of the variation in wellbeing. In the US data comes out nearly as large; in the UK data is larger. and are several times larger. At \beta \approx -0.05 the scatter looks like no relationship at all. Averages can hide larger effects for some people, and these surveys measure time, not doomscrolling.

Next, dopamine. A common claim is that phones flood the brain with it "like a drug". The best-known human measurement is a 1998 study in which eight men played a tank video game during a PET scan . The scan measures a radioactive tracer binding to dopamine receptors; released dopamine takes up receptors, so less tracer binds. Switch between this indirect measure in people and direct measurements in animals:

On the the game's average 13% drop resembles a low intravenous dose of amphetamine, a comparison the original paper made. But the game's range ran from an 8% rise to a 42% drop across eight people, against scan noise of 4 to 6%, and head movement and blood-flow changes during a task can mimic the signal . Converting binding to dopamine is also uncertain: the authors estimated "at least a doubling", but in monkeys the same drop in binding went with very different dopamine increases .

The are clearer. During sex, dopamine in a rat's nucleus accumbens roughly doubles ; a small intravenous dose of amphetamine raised a monkey's striatal dopamine more than fivefold, and twice the dose about fifteenfold. Natural rewards release dopamine; that is what the system is for. No measurement of feeds supports "like cocaine", and the drug comparisons that exist mix methods that do not convert cleanly.

"Phones are as addictive as drugs because of dopamine." Every rewarding experience involves dopamine release, so dopamine alone cannot tell a habit from an addiction. Problematic social media use is studied, and some people clearly struggle with it, but "social media addiction" is not a diagnosis in the main psychiatric manuals, and the dopamine numbers do not show a drug-like surge.

Finally, the strongest evidence: experiments that randomly assign people to cut back. In one, 143 students limited to ten minutes a day per platform for three weeks reported less loneliness and depression than controls . Larger trials paid people to deactivate Facebook or Instagram for weeks before US elections:

The big trials agree: weeks without Facebook or Instagram make people slightly happier, by 0.04 to 0.09 standard deviations , real but a fraction of what does on average. A meta-analysis of 27 experiments found a similar average with an interval that includes zero , and which experiments belong in such a pool is disputed. One detail stands out: weeks after the 2018 trial, people who had deactivated still used Facebook about 11 minutes a day less. A break loosens the habit.

Flat rewards work because a perfectly predictable feed stops producing prediction errors after a few posts, so nothing renews the pull. shortens sessions a little, and leaves sessions alone but starts fewer of them.

The real evidence is thinner than the model's tidy histogram. Students who set their phones to grayscale for about a week cut daily screen time by roughly 38 minutes . In a field experiment with 237 people, getting notifications in three batches a day lowered stress and raised wellbeing, while turning them off entirely raised anxiety . Personal versions of the same ideas: a phone charging outside the bedroom puts travel time back into the patch, and app limits give the feed an end.

If scrolling is regularly costing you sleep, or leaving you anxious or low, or getting in the way of work or school, that is worth raising with a doctor or a mental-health professional.

Summary

Doomscrolling is neither a mystery nor a drug addiction. Dopamine neurons signal outcomes better than expected; a feed that pays rarely and at random keeps that signal coming and hides when the payoff stops. Repetition turns checking into a habit. Bad news gets help: threats grab attention, outrage spreads, each frightening check raises the next question, and the feed never ends. At night the main cost is lost time.

Effects on wellbeing are tiny in surveys and small in trials, the drug comparisons rest on indirect measures, and willpower does not run out like fuel. What remains is a well-understood mechanism for keeping people looking, aimed by fixable design choices at the worst news on the worst nights. The most promising fixes change the situation, not the person: give the feed an end.
