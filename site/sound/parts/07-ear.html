The ear

All of this ends at the ear. The outer ear funnels sound into the ear canal, a tube about 2.5 cm long sealed at its far end by the eardrum. Behind the eardrum, in a small air-filled cavity, three tiny bones, the ossicles, carry the vibration to the cochlea, a coiled tube of fluid buried in the skull, where it becomes nerve signals. The motion is enlarged enormously here. At the loudness of conversation the eardrum moves by a few billionths of a meter. Why the bones at all? Because the cochlea is full of fluid, and sound in air bounces off water almost completely. Air is light and gives way easily; water is dense and stiff, so a pressure that sets air moving barely moves water. Of the energy in a sound wave striking a water surface, only about a tenth of a percent gets in. The middle ear has two tricks to do better. Change them:

The eardrum has about 17 times the area of the stirrup's footplate, so the force it collects is concentrated on a patch 17 times smaller, and the pressure rises 17-fold. The hammer and anvil add a lever of about 1.3. Together they raise the pressure about 22 times, some 27 dB , which in this simple model lets around 40% of the energy into the fluid instead of 0.1%. A would need about 60 times. When the middle ear fails, from fluid behind the eardrum in a child's ear infection or bones fused by disease, hearing drops by tens of decibels.

Inside the cochlea, running its whole length, is the basilar membrane, a strip of tissue that carries about 3,500 rows of hair cells. Unroll the cochlea and pick a frequency:

The membrane is narrow and stiff at the base, by the stirrup, and about five times wider and far floppier at the apex. So every stretch of it is tuned to a different frequency, from 20 kHz at the base to 20 Hz at the apex. Georg von Békésy watched the result in cochleas from cadavers : a tone sends a ripple along the membrane that grows until it reaches the place tuned to it, then dies out abruptly.In a living ear the outer hair cells push on the membrane in time with the sound, making the peaks far taller and sharper than in Békésy's cadavers. Each tone sets its own stretch of membrane moving, and the hair cells there fire. The cochlea does in fluid and tissue what the spectrum did on paper: it splits a sound into its frequencies, which is why you hear the harmonics and not the shape of the wave. Tones stir overlapping stretches and blur into beats or roughness; tones far apart are heard as two. And because every sound passes the base on its way in, the hair cells there take the most wear: hearing loss from age and noise begins with the highest pitches.

The cochlea is a spectrum analyzer: each place along it answers to one frequency.

Clap your hands once more. The squeeze leaves your palms at 343 m/s, handed from molecule to molecule while each one only rocks in place. It spreads and fades by 6 dB for every doubling of distance, bounces off the walls, and reaches your ear a few milliseconds later as a pressure wiggle of a fraction of a pascal. The eardrum follows it, three bones turn it into a push on fluid, and a ripple runs along the cochlea. A clap holds every frequency at once, so the whole membrane stirs, base first, and within a few milliseconds your brain knows what made the sound and where.
