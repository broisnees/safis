Pipes, resonance, and the voice

Wind instruments use the same trick with air. In a pipe the air itself sloshes back and forth, and the ends set the rules. At an open end the air moves freely, but its pressure is held at the room's, so the pressure cannot swing there. At a closed end the air cannot move at all. The even harmonics vanish and the lowest note drops an octave. That is why a clarinet, about as long as a flute, plays almost an octave lower, and why its low notes sound hollow, like the square wave.

Strings, pipes, and solid objects all have frequencies they prefer. Tap a wine glass and it rings at its own. Now push it with sound instead: a tone whose pressure pushes the rim back and forth. Sweep the frequency of the push past the glass's own, here 660 Hz, and watch how the rim responds:

This is resonance. The sharpness Q sets how tall and narrow the peak is: something that loses little energy each cycle rings long after a tap, and responds hugely, but only near its own frequency.

Your voice uses resonance too. The vocal folds in your larynx snap open and shut about 100 to 250 times a second, making a buzz rich in harmonics. Above them sits the vocal tract, a tube about 17 cm long, closed at the folds and open at the lips. Like the clarinet it resonates at a quarter wavelength and its odd multiples, about 500, 1,500, and 2,500 Hz for a relaxed "uh", boosting the harmonics near them .
