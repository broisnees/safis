What sets the speed

If the push is passed along by molecules hitting molecules, it cannot outrun them. Heat a gas and its molecules move faster, so the push should travel faster too. A light gas, whose molecules move faster at the same temperature, should carry sound faster than a heavy one. Drag the temperature and switch gases:

Air at room temperature carries sound at 343 m/s, about a kilometer in three seconds, which is why you can count the seconds between lightning and thunder and divide by three for the distance in kilometers. Its molecules average about 500 m/s, so sound runs at roughly two thirds of their speed in every gas, at every temperature. Helium, whose atoms are seven times lighter than an air molecule, carries sound almost three times faster; heavy carbon dioxide carries it slower.

The formula behind the curves reads the same story in symbols:

c = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma\, R\, \orange{T}}{M}}

Here \orange{T} is the absolute temperature, M the molar mass, R the gas constant, and \gamma a number near 1.4 for air that accounts for the gas heating up a little as each squeeze passes . Pressure appears nowhere. That should be surprising, since the squeezing force in our bead model was the pressure.

An airliner cruises at 11 km, where the air pressure is less than a quarter of what it is on the ground. How fast does sound travel up there?

Thin air pushes back with less pressure, but it is lighter by exactly the same factor, so stiffness over density does not change. Only temperature is left, and at 11 km it is −56 °C.

Climb through the standard atmosphere and watch the three curves. Drag the altitude :

From the ground to 11 km the pressure falls to a fifth, the temperature falls by 71 degrees, and the speed of sound drops from 340 to 295 m/s, tracking the temperature. Above 11 km the temperature stops falling. The pressure keeps dropping, by half again at 16 km, yet . That flat stretch is the clean experiment: pressure changes by a factor of three, the speed of sound by nothing.

The reason is the one in the quiz. Thinner air pushes back more weakly when squeezed, but there is proportionally less of it to set moving, so the ratio of stiffness to density, and with it the speed, does not change. What the molecules carry the push with is their own speed, and that depends only on temperature.

Sound is not faster in a material because the material is denser. Density slows sound down and stiffness speeds it up. Water and steel carry sound quickly because they are vastly stiffer than air, not because they are heavier.

Why γ is in the formula 2 min

Newton worked out the speed of sound in 1687 by treating air as a spring whose stiffness is its pressure. He got about 290 m/s and could not explain why measurements gave over 340. The gap is the factor \sqrt{\gamma} \approx 1.18, found by Laplace in 1816.

Each squeeze in a sound wave happens too fast for heat to leak away, so the compressed air warms slightly and pushes back harder than it would at constant temperature; each stretch cools it. A squeeze without heat exchange is called adiabatic, and its stiffness is \gamma P instead of P, with \gamma = 1.4 for air (a gas of two-atom molecules) and 1.67 for helium (single atoms). Dividing by the density \rho = PM/RT gives the formula above, with the pressure cancelling.

A wave of pressure

Back to a steady tone. In the first figure, the pressure curve and the displacement curve were shifted against each other by a quarter of a wavelength. That shift is the most confusing thing about sound waves, so it is worth a figure of its own. Drag the probe along the air and compare what the parcels are doing with the pressure there:

Put the probe where the pressure curve peaks. The parcels on the left of the probe are moving right and the parcels on its right are moving left: they are closing in, so the air between them is being squeezed. Where the displacement is largest the parcels on both sides are pushed the same way by the same amount, so nothing is squeezed and the pressure is normal. Pressure tracks how quickly displacement changes from one parcel to the next, not how big the displacement is.

Because the air moves along the same line the wave travels, this is called a longitudinal wave, unlike a wave on a rope, where the rope moves sideways. A microphone, and your eardrum, respond to the pressure, so from now on the red pressure curve is the one we will plot.

Pitch is how often

The loudspeaker's cone moved back and forth 220 times a second, and the air delivered 220 squeezes a second to anything in its path. That rate is the frequency, and our ears hear it as pitch. Because every squeeze travels at the same 343 m/s, a higher frequency packs them closer together in space. Sweep the frequency from the lowest note of a pipe organ to the edge of human hearing, and press play to hear it:

At 440 Hz, the A above middle C, the squeezes arrive every 2.3 ms and sit 78 cm apart in the air. The lowest organ pipes make waves over 10 m long, longer than most rooms; the highest sounds a young adult can hear, around 17 000 Hz, make waves 2 cm long, shorter than a fingertip. That thousandfold range in size is why low notes bend around corners and through walls while high notes cast sound shadows: an obstacle only blocks a wave that is small compared with the obstacle.

Sweeping the slider also shows something about the ear. The step from 110 to 220 Hz and the step from 1760 to 3520 Hz sound like the same jump in pitch, although one is 110 Hz and the other sixteen times larger. We hear ratios of frequency, not differences. Play two notes on a keyboard and compare:

Every C on the keyboard has exactly twice the frequency of the C below it: C3 is 130.8 Hz, C4 261.6 Hz, C5 523.3 Hz. Two notes an octave apart sound like the same note, higher. Between the Cs, the twelve keys of an octave split the doubling into twelve equal ratios of about 1.059 each. On the frequency axis under the keys, those equal musical steps get wider and wider in hertz; to the ear they are all the same size. The ear works in ratios, and it will again.
