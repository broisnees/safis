• A transformer cuts text into tokens, turns each token into a list of numbers, passes the lists up through a stack of identical layers, and turns the last one into a probability for every possible next token.
  • In each layer, attention moves information between positions and a small network, the MLP, works on each position alone. Both add their results to a running total, the residual stream.
  • Training sets every number by making the next token of real text a little more probable, from the 8,204 numbers here to hundreds of billions.
  • Every figure runs a real, tiny transformer on the prompt you give it.

Type the start of a sentence into a chatbot and it carries on, one piece at a time, each piece chosen after looking at everything before it. The program doing the choosing is almost always a transformer, a design published in 2017 for translation that now runs nearly every large language model. Its parts are few and each is simple arithmetic; what makes it hard to picture is that there are billions of them.

So I built a small one: two layers, two attention heads per layer, 16 numbers per word, 8,204 learned numbers in all. It was trained, by the same code that runs a live training run further down, on 3,000 sentences of a made-up language with 44 wordsThe whole vocabulary: the . is are near that ate drank sat ran slept on to in cat dog bird girl boy cats dogs birds girls boys mat park tree house garden river fish bread milk water happy tired hungry small big old saw chased liked found. Sentences come from a small grammar, written out in the page source.: cats drink milk, dogs chase cats, the birds near the house are hungry. Type a prompt from those words and watch it write:

Start with and it writes are, then a describing word and a full stop. The word just before the gap is dog, which would take is; the model looks past it to cats. Try and it switches to is, at 98%. Nobody wrote a rule about subjects and verbs. The model learned one, and by the end of this page we'll have found the machinery that does it.

Drawing at random sometimes picks an unlikely word and the sentence goes wrong; likeliest plays safe. Either way the loop is the one on the page about next-word prediction: compute a probability for every token, pick one, append it, repeat. This page is about what happens inside one step, and how the numbers that do it were learned.

Tokens

The model never sees letters. A prompt is first cut into pieces called tokens, and each token is replaced by its number in the vocabulary: the cats near the dog becomes 0, 19, 4, 0, 15. Our toy cuts at spaces and drops any word outside its 44.

Real models can't afford to drop anything, so they build their vocabularies with byte-pair encoding . Start from single characters. Count every pair of neighbors, glue the commonest pair into a new token, and repeat. The figure below learns merges from whatever text is in the box, so edit it to anything you like, and step through the merges:

At zero merges every character is a token, spaces included (drawn as dots). The first merges glue a and t, which sit together 12 times, then t and h, then th and e, then a space onto the. By merge the common words are single tokens with their leading space attached, ·the, ·cat, ·sat, and 106 characters have become 52 tokens. Rare words stay in pieces, and an unseen word still comes out as something, because single characters are always in the vocabulary. Real tokenizers do this on raw bytes over huge amounts of text and stop after tens of thousands of merges; GPT-2 has 50,257 tokens .

Every merged token remembers the two pieces it was made from, so each one is the top of a little tree whose leaves are characters. Pick a token:

·the is four characters joined by three merges, in the order the pairs were common. Trees like these are why a model sees ·cat and ·cats as two unrelated numbers and has to learn that they are related, and why language models are oddly bad at counting letters: the letters are sealed inside tokens they never see opened.Ask a model how many r's are in strawberry and it has to answer from what it learned about a few tokens, not by looking at ten letters.

Vectors

A number like 19 says nothing about cats. The model's first real step turns each token into a list of 16 numbers, a vector, by looking it up in a table: the embedding table, which has one column per token in the vocabulary. Pick a token, or click a column:

is column 14. Its nearest neighbors, measured by the cosine of the angle between columns, are boy, girl and bird, and the patterns look alike even by eye. sits near river, tree and house, near found and liked. Nobody told the model which words are animals: the table started random, and training pushed words used in the same places toward the same patterns, the effect the attention page builds from counting.

Sixteen numbers make each word a point in a space with 16 directions. We can't see that, but we can find the three directions in which the 44 points spread most and look at their shadows there. Turn the cloud:

The groups appear without anyone drawing them: animals in one bunch, places in another, food and describing words on one side, and saw, chased, liked, found together. The arrows from each singular noun to its plural point roughly the same way, so somewhere in the 16 numbers is a direction that means "more than one". The model needs it: it's what it reads to choose between is and are.

These three directions hold only about half of the spread, so the picture is a shadow. Large models use thousands of numbers per token, with room for far more distinctions.

A table lookup can't capture where a word is: the dog chased the cat and the cat chased the dog contain the same five vectors, and attention by itself ignores orderThe attention page shows this with dog bites man: without positions, a head can't tell the two sentences apart.. So every transformer mixes position into its vectors. The original design added a fixed pattern , which makes most sense as a row of clocks: each pair of numbers is one hand, and each hand turns at its own speed as you move along the text. Move along the text, and add an offset:

The fastest hand turns 57° per position, the slowest less than a tenth of a degree, and together they give every position its own picture, as hour, minute and second hands do for every moment of a day. The useful property is in the ghost hands: moving positions later turns each hand by its own fixed angle, wherever you start, so dragging the position leaves the arcs the same size. A fixed turn is a fixed linear operation, so "three tokens back" is the same arithmetic everywhere, which a layer can learn once.

Many recent models drop the added pattern and use the clocks more directly. Rotary embeddings rotate the two vectors attention is about to compare, a query and a key, each by an angle proportional to its own position. Here are a 2-number query and key, rotated 20° per position. Move them along the text, then try against :

Both pairs are three apart, and both score 0.93. Move both positions together and the arrows turn together, so the angle between them, and the score, stays put: it depends only on the distance between the tokens, usually what matters in language. Real rotary embeddings do this with many pairs at once, turning at the clocks' different speeds.

Our toy uses neither: it learns a second table, one vector per position, added to the word's vector, as GPT-2 did . Either way, each token now enters the model as one vector saying what it is and roughly where. That vector is the token's residual stream, a name that will make sense shortly.

One head

Back to the opening puzzle. At dog the model must produce are, and the reason, cats, sits three positions back in a different stream. Moving information between positions is attention's job. The attention page builds it from hand-set numbers; here we take apart a learned one, the second head of layer 2, which I'll call head 2.2, because it turns out to solve the puzzle.

The head starts by making three new vectors for every token, each by multiplying the token's stream (after a normalization step we'll meet later) by a matrix: a query, a key, and a value, 8 numbers each. Each matrix is 16 numbers tall and 8 wide. Watch the result fill in, one number at a time, or point at any cell:

Each number of a query is a dot product of one token's 16 normalized numbers with one column of the matrix. The whole table is one matrix multiplication, \orange{Q} = X\orange{W_Q}, and the same rows times two other matrices give \blue{K} = X\blue{W_K} and \green{V} = X\green{W_V}. Switch between them: the rows never change, so each token gets three descriptions made from the same numbers. Roughly, the query says what a position is looking for, the key what it can be found by, and the value what it hands over. Those readings are a convenience; training shaped the 384 numbers only to make predictions better.

Next, every query meets every key. Their dot product, divided by \sqrt{8} to keep it from growing with the length of the vectors, is the score. Each row of scores then goes through softmax, which turns it into weights that are positive and add up to one. Turn the causal mask off, and change the temperature the scores are divided by:

In the bottom row, the query of dog scores highest against the key of cats, 1.6, and below zero against everything else, so softmax gives cats 85% of the weight. The rows for cats and near point there too. Lower the temperature and each row's top score takes nearly everything; raise it and the weights flatten. The model itself always uses 1, and learned how sharp to be by making its scores bigger or smaller.

The hatched cells are the causal mask: a position may look only at itself and earlier positions, because when the model writes, later tokens don't exist yet. Switch it off and the upper corner fills with scores a writing model never computes. The mask has a second benefit, which we'll meet in training.

The weights only say how much to take from each position. What gets taken is the values. The head's output for a position is the weighted sum of every visible position's value, and an output matrix then turns those 8 numbers into 16 that are added to the position's stream. Watch it at the last position, or pick another:

At dog, rows with small weights fade to nothing and the sum is almost exactly the value of cats. The output matrix turns it into 16 numbers added to the stream of dog: whatever cats had to offer, including that it is plural, now sits where the verb gets chosen. Attention makes nothing new; it moves weighted copies between streams. The whole head, for every position at once, is one line :

\operatorname{head}(X) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\orange{Q}\blue{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{8}} + M\right)\green{V}\,W_O

Here M is the mask, zero where looking is allowed and minus infinity where it isn't.

A head is three matrices that turn each position into a query, a key and a value, a softmax that turns query-key matches into weights, and a weighted sum that copies values between positions.

Many heads

Each layer of our model has two heads side by side, each with its own matrices, their outputs added into the stream together. The original transformer had 8 heads per layer , GPT-3 96 in each of 96 layers . Here are all four of ours on . Point at a row to follow one position across the heads:

Each head has a habit. The first-layer heads mostly look at the position itself or a word or two back. Head 2.2 picks out the subject: from near, dog and happy it looks back at cats. Head 2.1 spreads its weight in a way that is hard to name, which is typical: in real models some heads have crisp jobs and many don't.Strictly, the heads' outputs are placed side by side, 8 + 8 numbers, and one 16 × 16 output matrix maps them back into the stream. That is the same as giving each head its own 8 × 16 slice of the matrix and adding the results, which is how the figure above it computed one head's share.

Where a head looks is a hint about what it does. A sharper test is to switch it off and see what breaks. Switch heads off:

Head 2.2 is the one that looks from dog back to cats. Switch it off: what happens to the prediction?

Knowing that a verb comes next doesn't need the subject; choosing its form does. Other parts of the model still recognize the verb slot, and only the copy of the subject goes missing.

Switch off and the model still expects is or are but can't tell which: it falls from 100% to 57%, little better than a coin toss, while its loss on text in general barely moves, from 1.13 to 1.15. Switch off and agreement doesn't change. Switch off and everything gets worse together, the loss rising to 1.78, because every later step was trained to read what the first layer writes.

The MLP

Attention only averages what the values already hold, so every layer has a second block that works on each position alone: a small two-layer network called the MLP, drawn in brass. It widens the 16 numbers to 64, bends each with a fixed curve, and narrows them back to 16. The widening by four is the original design's . The bend is essential: two matrix multiplications in a row are just another matrix multiplication, and couldn't compute anything new.

The original transformer bent with ReLU, which zeroes anything negative. GPT-2 and many later models use GELU , a smooth version that lets small negative values leak through. Behind the curves is a histogram of every number that actually arrives at our model's bend, measured on held-out text. Move the input along:

For large inputs the curves agree: positive numbers pass through, negative ones become about zero. Near zero GELU dips slightly, to −0.17 at an input of −0.75, then curves smoothly up. That is where the traffic is: 63% of the numbers our model sends through the bend are negative, most between −2 and 0. A smooth curve there gives training a slope to follow where ReLU gives a flat zero.Many recent models, LLaMA among them, use a gated variant called SwiGLU, in which one widened vector, bent, multiplies another element by element .

Here is the whole block at work on one position. The 16 numbers going in are the position's stream after normalization; each of the 64 hidden numbers is a dot product of them with one column of the first matrix, and the curve is applied to each. Pick a position and a layer:

Most of the 64 land below zero and come out as roughly nothing: at dog in layer 2 only 23 are above 0.1. Each hidden number is called a neuron, and the second matrix gives each its own 16-number vector to add to the stream, scaled by how strongly it fired. So the block is 64 tests on the incoming vector, each of which, when it passes, writes its own message.

So what does a neuron test for? I compared each neuron's firing, over a few hundred sentences, with simple facts about each position, and some match one closely. Choose a neuron:

Neuron 60 of layer 1 fires on the last word before a full stop: happy, milk, birds, small. Across the test sentences its firing tracks "a full stop comes next" with a correlation of 0.93. Neuron 45 of layer 2 fires on a the where an animal is likely to come next: at the start of a sentence, after near or that, after chased, but hardly after drank or in; its correlation with "an animal comes next" is 0.91. In layer 2, neuron 48 fires on is and are, where a describing word comes next. Each, when it fires, adds a vector that pushes up the scores of the words that fit.

Studies of real models, with tens of thousands of neurons per layer, find many that respond to recognizable patterns and push up the words that tend to follow, which is why MLPs are sometimes described as memories keyed by patterns . Most neurons are less tidy and respond to several unrelated things; the three above are the clearest of our 128.

The residual stream

Both blocks end the same way: they add their result to the position's vector instead of replacing it. That running vector is the residual stream, drawn in purple. It starts as embedding plus position, and every block adds a contribution, so the final vector is literally a sum. Watch one position's stream as the blocks add to it, drawn as an arrow; the line under it is what the model would predict if it stopped there:

At dog, the bare embedding predicts to, a guess about what follows a noun. Attention 1 brings in enough of cats to favor are; MLP 1 muddies it to a near tie, still 50 to 47 after attention 2; MLP 2 settles it at 99%. The stream grows from a length of about 1 to 8.5, since every block adds. Reading the stream partway like this is called the logit lens , and in large models it shows the same thing: a rough guess early, refined by later layers.

Adding is the key design decision. Each block contributes a correction rather than rebuilding the vector, and whatever an early block writes stays available to every later one. The idea came from image networks, where such skip connections let people train networks hundreds of layers deep .

The growing length is a problem, though: a block in layer 30 would see vectors many times longer than one in layer 2. So each block reads a normalized copy of the stream: subtract its average, divide by its spread, then scale and shift each number by a learned gain and bias. That is layer normalization . Drag the bars, or use the buttons:

Double every one of the eight numbers. What happens to the normalized output?

Doubling doubles both the distances from the mean and the spread, and the division cancels them.

Either button moves the input and leaves the output exactly where it was: normalization keeps only the vector's shape, which entries are high and which low, and every output has average 0 and spread 1. The learned gain and bias then let a block make some directions louder than others. Our model normalizes the copy each block reads, never the stream itself, as GPT-2 did , plus once more before the output.The original transformer normalized after each addition instead. Putting it at the start of each block made deep models easier to train, and most models since do it this way. Many also skip subtracting the mean, a cheaper variant called RMSNorm .

Depth

A block can only use what is already in the streams, so a second layer can look for things no single token's vector contains. The clearest example is a trick real models learn early in training, the induction head : when the text repeats something, continue it the way it went last time.

To show it with nothing hidden I built a two-layer model by hand, on random letters. Its stream has four labeled parts: which letter this is, which position, a slot for the previous letter, and a slot for the prediction. The first layer has one head whose query at position t asks for position t-1; its value copies that position's letter into the previous-letter slot. Step along the text:

After layer 1, each stream holds its own letter and the letter before it, a combination no embedding contains. The second layer's head uses it: its query is the position's own letter and its key reads the previous-letter slot, so a position finds the places right after an earlier copy of itself; its value copies the letter found there into the prediction slot. Step through the second half, then switch the first layer off:

In the first half nothing matches, so the head puts its weight on an empty default slotTrained models have the same need for somewhere to put attention when nothing matches, and often use the first token of the text for it., copies nothing, and the prediction is a blur. From the first repeat on, the head jumps to the position after the earlier copy and predicts the letter that followed last time: five of five right. Switch off layer 1 and every key is zero, nothing matches anywhere, and the predictions blur again. The second layer's question only has an answer because the first layer wrote it.

Trained models build this circuit themselves, from two-layer attention-only models to large ones, and its appearance during training coincides with a jump in learning from examples in the prompt . Our agreement head is a milder case of the same thing: switch off a first-layer head and head 2.2 loses the subject, as showed. Real models stack many layers:

The output

After the last block, the stream of the last position is normalized one final time and turned into a score for every token in the vocabulary. The step is the reverse of the embedding lookup: an unembedding matrix with one 16-number column per token, and each token's score, its logit, is the dot product of the stream with that token's column. Point at a word's bar to compare its column with the stream:

For the cats near the dog, the column of are lines up with the stream, so the products are mostly positive and the score is about 10. The column of is disagrees in a few telling places and scores about 5, and a gap of 5.5 in score is a factor of e^{5.5} \approx 250 in probability. Predicting a word means the stream has been steered, block by block, to point the way that word's column points.

Scores are not probabilities: they can be any size and sign. Softmax turns them into probabilities, as in attention, and then one token has to be chosen. The next-word page walks through the choices; here they are on our model's real probabilities. Pick a prompt, a temperature, and a rule, then draw 200 times:

After the boys ran to the, four places share the probability almost evenly, and 200 draws land within a few percent of the red bars. Lower the temperature and the leader pulls away; raise it and words that never belong there start getting drawn. Top-2 keeps the two likeliest words; top-50% keeps the fewest words whose probabilities reach 50%, one after the cat sat on the but two after the boys ran to the . Greedy always takes the top word. None of these changes what the model computed, only which guess gets written down.

Training

Every number we've looked at started as a random number. Training changes them to make the actual next token in real text more probable. The penalty for one prediction is -\ln p, where p is the probability the model gave the token that really came next: 0 if certain and right, large if confident and wrong. The page on learning covers this loss and gradient descent. What is special about a transformer is how many predictions one pass yields. Here is held-out text, with the probability the model gave each actual next token:

The trained model is nearly certain where the grammar leaves no choice, like the after on or a full stop after a describing word, and pays most where the language is random, at the choice of the next animal or food. The untrained model gives everything roughly 1 in 44 and pays close to \ln 44 \approx 3.8 per token, the price of guessing blind. Here the causal mask pays off: since no position sees the future, one pass over 16 tokens checks 16 predictions at once. Real models check thousands per pass, a large part of why transformers train so much faster than the recurrent networks they replaced.

Training nudges all 8,204 numbers a little in the direction that would have lowered the loss, found by backpropagation, and repeats on new text. Below, a fresh copy of the model starts from the same random numbers and trains in your browser, with the code that made this page's weights. Every 25 steps it is tested on held-out text and asked to continue the cats near the dog:

At step 0 it repeats whatever word its random numbers favor. By step 100 the loss is below 2 and by about 150 it writes sentences; getting are right comes much later, near step 1,000. The held-out dots track the training curve, because 3,000 sentences of a simple grammar are hard to memorize with 8,204 numbers. A larger step size falls faster and stays noisy; a smaller one crawls.

How good can a model get by being bigger? In 2020 a group at OpenAI trained transformers from 768 to 1.5 billion numbers (not counting the embeddings) and found that, given enough data, the held-out loss fell along a straight line on a log-log plot, a power law . Slide the model size along their fitted line:

The line is straight over six orders of magnitude, from models smaller than ours to GPT-2's size: every doubling cuts the loss by the same 5%, 2^{-0.076} \approx 0.95. It is an empirical fit, on one kind of text and one family of models, and its numbers don't carry over to our made-up language. But it held well enough to plan with, and gave a reason to build models a thousand times larger.

A bigger model also needs more text, and the compute to train it is roughly 6 operations per parameter per token, C \approx 6ND. For a fixed budget, then, there is a trade between parameters N and tokens D. In 2022 a team at DeepMind trained over 400 models to find the best split and concluded that the two should grow together, at about 20 tokens per parameter . Set a compute budget:

At the budget of Gopher and Chinchilla, about 5.8 \times 10^{23} operations, the optimum is 70 billion parameters on 1.4 trillion tokens, the model DeepMind then trained. Chinchilla, a quarter of Gopher's size trained on 4.7 times as much text, beat Gopher and GPT-3 across a wide range of tests. The earlier models sit right of the dotted line, too big for the text they saw. LLaMA 7B sits far left on purpose, at 150 tokens per parameter : extra training, paid once, buys a smaller model that is cheaper every time it runs. Our toy read its 20,000-token corpus about 50 times; at its size the rule was never measured.

Counting a transformer's numbers 2 min

Per layer, attention has four d \times d matrices (query, key, value, output), 4d^2 numbers. The MLP has two matrices of d \times 4d, another 8d^2. Layer norms and biases add only a few times d. So a model with L layers has about

N \approx 12\,L\,d^2

numbers outside its embedding tables. For GPT-3, 12 \times 96 \times 12{,}288^2 \approx 1.74 \times 10^{11}: the advertised 175 billion. For our model, 12 \times 2 \times 16^2 = 6{,}144, plus 352 biases and gains, plus 1,708 in the embedding, position and unembedding tables, gives the 8,204. The same count explains C \approx 6ND: each parameter costs one multiply and one add per token going forward, and about twice that going backward.

The cost of writing

Writing can't run in parallel the way training does: the model needs token 6 before it can compute position 7, so generation is a loop of one full pass per token. Much of each pass can be saved. Because of the causal mask, earlier positions' keys and values never change, so a model keeps them, the key–value cache, and for each new token computes only its own query, key and value. Watch head 2.2's cache fill as the model writes:

Without the cache, writing token n recomputes the keys and values of all n tokens so far, and the total grows with the square of the length: 272 vectors for 16 tokens, against 32 with the cache. The price is memory. A model the shape of LLaMA 7B keeps a key and a value of 4,096 numbers in each of its 32 layers for every token: at two bytes a number, half a megabyte per token, 2 gigabytes for a 4,096-token conversation.

A model doesn't read the prompt and then write. Every token it writes runs through every layer again, one at a time; the cache only saves recomputing the past. That is why long answers take visibly longer to produce than short ones.

With the cache, double the length of the text so far. How much more attention work does the next token need?

The new token's query is compared with every cached key, one comparison each, so twice the keys means twice the work. Summed over every token of the text, that is four times as much.

The cache doesn't remove the other cost. Each new token's query still has to be compared with every cached key, and its weights used on every cached value, so attention's work per token grows in proportion to the length of the text so far, while the work in the matrices stays fixed. Choose a model shape and slide the context length:

For a model the shape of LLaMA 7B, attention is a small share of the work for short texts and overtakes the matrices at about 24,600 tokens, six times its width; at a million tokens it is 98% of the work and the cache needs about 500 gigabytes. Summed over a whole text, the comparisons grow with the square of its length, the cost the attention page ends on. Hence the long-standing limits on context length, and much engineering: attention computed in tiles that fit in fast memory, keys and values shared among query heads, or each token attending to only part of the past.

From toy to real

Chatbots run this same pass, at another scale and with refinements: tens to hundreds of layers, thousands of numbers per token, rotary positions, gated MLPs, trillions of training tokens. Chat models are then trained further on examples of helpful answers and on people's preferences between answers, which shapes the continuations they favor. The machine stays the same: tokens become vectors, attention moves information between positions, MLPs transform each position, everything adds into a residual stream, and the last stream becomes a probability for every next token.

What no one can yet do is read a large model the way we read this one. Our agreement head and full-stop neuron were easy to find among four heads and 128 neurons. A large model has tens of thousands of heads and millions of neurons, most doing several jobs at once, and working out which numbers do what is a research field of its own. The arithmetic is all on this page; what it adds up to at that scale is still being worked out.
