How Spinning Circles Draw Anything

How any repeating wave, and even any closed drawing, can be built from circles turning at whole-number speeds, and how to find out which circles a shape needs.

  • A dot going round a circle traces a sine wave with its height. The circle's size, speed, and starting angle are the wave's amplitude, frequency, and phase.
  • Stack circles on circles and the heights add. With whole-number speeds the result repeats every turn, and enough circles can match any repeating wave, even a square one, except for an overshoot of about 9% of each jump that never goes away.
  • To find how much of each speed a wave holds, multiply it by a test wave of that speed and average. Every other speed averages to zero.
  • Let the circles turn both ways and the pen can draw any closed shape in the plane. Most shapes need only their few biggest circles, which is the idea behind compressing sound and pictures.

Pluck a guitar string and hum the same note, and you hear two different sounds with the same pitch. Draw a cat on a napkin, and you have a shape no formula seems to describe. Both, it turns out, can be built out of the simplest motion there is: a point going round a circle at a steady speed. Put a circle on the rim of a circle, another on the rim of that, and so on, give each one a whole-number speed, and the last point can trace any wave that repeats and any closed drawing.

Here is an eighth note drawn that way. The pen is the blue dot at the end of a chain of turning circles, and the slider sets how many circles the chain has. Slide it down to a handful and watch the note melt, then back up:

With one circle the pen just goes round. With five it draws a loop that only hints at a flag; with ten the head, stem, and flag are there but soft; with forty the note is crisp, and with a hundred and twenty the copy sits on the dashed original. Every circle in that chain turns at a steady whole-number speed: once per lap, twice, three times, some clockwise and some counterclockwise. Nothing else moves.

The idea behind this goes back to Joseph Fourier, who used it in 1807 to work out how heat spreads through a solid . It now runs inside every phone: in how sound is recorded and squeezed into small files, how photos are stored, and how radios pick one station out of the air. I'll build it up from a single circle.

One circle

Take one circle and a dot that goes round it at a steady speed, counterclockwise. Ignore everything about the dot except its height above the center line. Press play, or drag the dot, and watch its height drawn out over time on the right:

The curve on the right is a sine wave. It isn't a curve that happens to look like the circle. It is the circle, unrolled along time: the top of the circle is the crest, the bottom is the trough, and the steep middle of the wave is where the dot moves straight up or down past the center line. One turn of the dot draws one complete wave, and the next turn draws the same wave again.

I'll measure time by how far a dot of speed 1 has turned, in radians, so one full turn takes 2π. That's why the time axis is marked in multiples of π. The height at time t is then just \sin t, which is what the sine button on a calculator gives.

Three things set a wave like this. The first two are the size of the circle and how fast the dot goes round. Make the radius bigger or smaller, and step the speed up to several turns per lap, keeping an eye on the dashed speed-1 wave for comparison:

The radius is the wave's amplitude, how far it rises above the middle line. The speed is its frequency, how many complete waves fit into the same time. At speed 3 the dot goes round three times while the dashed wave makes one, so three crests sit where the dashed wave has one. A wave with radius \orange{r} and speed k has height \orange{r}\sin(kt).

The third thing is where the dot starts. So far every dot set off from the right-hand side of its circle, at height zero, heading up. Turn the knob to start the dot further round, at the brass mark, and compare the new wave with the dashed one that starts at zero:

The starting angle is the wave's phase. Moving the start further round doesn't change the wave's shape at all. It slides the wave earlier in time by exactly the angle you turned, because the dot reaches every point that much sooner. Start it , at the top, and the wave begins at its crest: that's the cosine wave, \cos t. Sine and cosine aren't two different shapes. They are one circle, watched from two starting points.

A circle and a dot make a wave. The radius is its amplitude, the speed its frequency, and the starting angle its phase. Those three numbers describe a sine wave completely.

Circles on circles

One circle makes one smooth wave. To make anything more interesting, put a second, smaller circle on the rim of the first, with its own dot going round at its own speed. The first dot now carries the second circle around with it, and the pen is the dot on the second circle. Step the second circle's speed up and change its size, and watch how the blue height wave is built from the two thin ones:

The pen's height is the first dot's height plus the second dot's height above the first, so at every moment the blue wave is the gray wave plus the orange wave, added point by point. Where both are high, the sum is higher still; where one is up and the other down, they partly cancel. With a the big wave gets flattened shoulders. With it leans to one side like a shark fin. Two plain sine waves already make shapes that look nothing like sine waves.

Adding waves this way is called superposition, and it is what real waves do. Two ripples crossing on a pond, or two instruments playing at once, add their heights in exactly this way.

There is a reason I keep the speeds whole numbers. Look at the path the pen draws in the plane, not just its height. I'll make the second speed a slider that can stop between whole numbers. Watch whether the pen, after one full turn of the big circle, lands back on the orange ring where it started. The blue cross marks where the first turn ends:

At a whole-number speed like , the small circle makes exactly three turns while the big one makes one, so both are back where they started at the same moment, and the pen is on its ring. Then the whole motion repeats, exactly. At the small circle is half a turn off after the first lap, the pen misses, and it takes two laps to line up. At it takes five.

That is the whole reason for whole numbers. A wave that repeats every turn can only be made of circles that all come back to the start every turn, which means circles turning 1, 2, 3, or any whole number of times per turn. The slowest of them sets the beat, and is called the fundamental. The others, at whole-number multiples of its speed, are its harmonics. Every repeating wave on this page is built from a fundamental and its harmonics, and nothing else.

A square wave from smooth ones

The hardest test for this idea is a square wave: flat at +1 for half a turn, flat at −1 for the other half, with instant jumps between. It's what a switch flicked on and off at a steady beat produces. Every circle we have makes a smooth, round wave.

Can a sum of smooth sine waves make a wave with flat tops and sharp, vertical jumps?

With more and more waves the sum gets as close as you like to the flat tops, and the jumps get as steep as you like. But just beside each jump the sum always overshoots, by the same amount no matter how many waves you add.

The recipe, which I'll show how to find in a later section, uses only odd speeds: 1, 3, 5, 7, and so on, with the wave of speed n made n times smaller than the first. Add the waves one at a time with the stepper. The orange wave is the one just added, and the blue wave is the sum so far. Press Play to hear the sum as a note while you add waves:

The first wave is a rough guess: right sign, wrong shape. The is a third as tall, and it pushes the sum down at the middle of each half and up near the edges, flattening the top. The flattens it more. By the tops are almost level and the jumps almost vertical. Each wave is smaller and faster than the one before, so it fixes a finer detail.

Here is the same sum built from circles. Each odd speed gets its own circle, stacked on the one before, with the newest in orange. Add circles and watch the pen's height sharpen into a square wave:

The big circle does most of the work, carrying the pen up for half a turn and down for the other half. Each smaller circle rides on the rim of the one before and, because it turns three, five, or seven times as fast, swings the pen a little up or down at just the right moments to flatten the top. Nothing in the chain ever stops or jumps. Every circle turns steadily, and yet the pen's height jumps.

Now look closely at the corners. Near each jump the blue sum doesn't stop at 1: it overshoots, rings, and settles. It's natural to expect the overshoot to fade as waves are added. Drag the number of waves up into the hundreds and watch the red line, which marks how high the first peak goes:

The ringing gets narrower as waves are added, squeezing in toward the jump, but it doesn't get shorter. At the first peak reaches 1.180; at it still reaches 1.179. The right-hand view stretches the region next to the jump so the ringing always fills the same width, and there it hardly changes at all. The overshoot settles at about 9% of the full jump from −1 to +1, and stays there forever .

This is the Gibbs phenomenon. It doesn't contradict the claim that the sum becomes the square wave. Pick any moment away from the jump, and with enough waves the ringing moves past it and the sum there settles to exactly 1. The overshoot never dies; it just moves closer to the jump.Henry Wilbraham worked it out first, in 1848 . It turned up again fifty years later in an exchange of letters in Nature about adding up waves, which Albert Michelson, who had just built a machine that added them mechanically , took part in. J. Willard Gibbs described the overshoot in his letter of 1899, and Maxime Bôcher gave it Gibbs's name in 1906. Exactly at the jump, every sine wave is zero, so the sum lands halfway, at 0.

More terms don't remove the overshoot. A sum of smooth waves copying a sudden jump always rises about 9% of the jump too high just beside it. Only the width of the ringing shrinks.

Multiply and average

So far I've handed you the recipe. The real question runs the other way: given a wave, how do you find out which circles it's made of, and how big each one is? It sounds hopeless, since the waves are all added together into one curve. The trick that untangles them is to multiply the wave by a test wave and take the average over one turn.

Start with two plain sine waves, and multiply them point by point. Where both are positive or both negative, the product is positive; where they disagree, it's negative. Set the two speeds with the steppers and watch the purple product, its shaded area above and below zero, and its average:

With the product swings up and down, and the filled area above zero exactly balances the hatched area below, so the average is 0. Try any pair of different whole-number speeds and the balance holds every time. With the , the product is a sine wave times itself. It's never negative, it rocks between 0 and 1, and its average is exactly ½.

The reason is that the product of two waves is itself two waves, one at the sum of the speeds and one at the difference (\sin a \sin b = \tfrac12\cos(a-b) - \tfrac12\cos(a+b)). A wave of any whole-number speed makes whole turns in one lap and averages to zero, except one of speed zero, which is a constant. Only when the speeds match is the difference zero, and only then does something survive the average. Waves of different whole-number speeds are called orthogonal.

Now use it. Here is a wave made of a few sine waves of unknown sizes and speeds. Step the green test wave through speeds 1 to 8; at each one, the figure multiplies, averages, and doubles the result to undo the ½. The bars on the right keep a record of what you've measured:

At speed 3 the product balances out and the bar stays flat: there's no speed-3 wave inside. Step through the rest. Speeds and come out positive, 0.9 and 0.5; speed comes out at −0.3, which means that wave is upside down; everything else is zero. Those are exactly the numbers I used to build the hidden wave. The test wave of speed 2 is blind to the other components, because each of them is orthogonal to it.

The figure never reads the hidden numbers. It only multiplies and averages, over a thousand moments in one turn.

There's a catch. My hidden waves all started at height zero, like sines. A wave with a different phase gives a smaller average against the sine test, and a cosine gives nothing at all. The fix is to test with a cosine too. Turn the knob to slide the hidden wave's phase, and watch the two measured amounts, plotted as a point:

As the phase turns, the sine part shrinks while the cosine part grows, and the other way round, but the point they make never leaves the dashed circle. Its distance from the center is the wave's size, 0.8, whatever the phase. Its angle is the phase itself. So the two averages together pin down a wave completely: one number for how much, one for when it starts.

That is the full method. For each whole-number speed n, take twice the average of the wave times \green{\sin nt}, and twice the average of the wave times \green{\cos nt}:

b_n = 2\,\overline{f(t)\,\green{\sin nt}}, \qquad a_n = 2\,\overline{f(t)\,\green{\cos nt}}

The bar over a product means its average over one turn. Then the wave is the sum of all its pieces, f(t) = a_0 + \sum_n \left(a_n \cos nt + b_n \sin nt\right), where a_0 is the plain average of the wave, its middle level. This sum is called the wave's Fourier series.

Where the formula comes from, and the square wave's recipe 3 min

Suppose a wave really is a sum of sines and cosines at whole-number speeds:

f(t) = a_0 + \sum_{m \ge 1} \big(a_m \cos mt + b_m \sin mt\big)

Multiply both sides by \sin nt and average over one turn. The average of a sum is the sum of the averages, so each term can be averaged on its own. Using the product rules

\begin{aligned} \sin mt\,\sin nt &= \tfrac12\cos(m-n)t - \tfrac12\cos(m+n)t \\ \cos mt\,\sin nt &= \tfrac12\sin(n+m)t + \tfrac12\sin(n-m)t \end{aligned}

every term becomes waves of whole-number speed, which average to zero over a turn, except the single term \tfrac12 \cos 0 = \tfrac12 that appears when m = n in the first line. So the average of f(t)\sin nt is \tfrac12 b_n, and b_n is twice that average. Writing the average as an integral over one turn gives the form in textbooks:

b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(t)\sin nt\,dt

For the square wave, f = 1 on the first half turn and -1 on the second, so

b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\int_0^{\pi}\sin nt\,dt - \int_{\pi}^{2\pi}\sin nt\,dt\right) = \frac{2}{\pi n}\big(1 - \cos n\pi\big)

When n is even, \cos n\pi = 1 and the amount is zero. When it's odd, \cos n\pi = -1 and b_n = 4/(\pi n). That's the recipe from the square-wave section: odd speeds only, sizes falling as 1/n. The square wave's cosine amounts are all zero, because it is upside-down symmetric about t = 0 the same way a sine is.

Two cautions. This shows how to find the amounts if the wave is such a sum; that every reasonable repeating wave is one, with the sum settling to the wave's value wherever the wave doesn't jump, took most of the nineteenth century to prove properly, starting with Dirichlet in 1829 . And at a jump the sum settles halfway, as the square-wave figure showed.

The spectrum

Once the amounts are measured, the wave can be drawn a second way: not as height against time, but as a row of bars, one per speed, each as tall as the size of that speed's circle. That picture is the wave's spectrum. Pick a wave, and point at a bar to see the piece of the wave it stands for in orange:

The uses every speed, with sizes falling as 1/n. The uses odd speeds again, but its bars die away much faster. The , on for a sixth of each turn, has a bar at speed 0, its middle level of 1/6, and gaps at speeds 6, 12, and 18.

The spectrum and the wave hold the same information: given the bars, plus the phases I've left out of the picture, you can rebuild the wave exactly. One describes it in time, the other in speed.

Why do the triangle's bars shrink so much faster than the square's? The square jumps, and the triangle only has corners. Sharp features need fast waves to draw them. Smooth the square's jumps into ramps and watch the high speeds on this scale, where each step down is ten times smaller:

With a sharp the bars follow the upper guide, 1/n: the speed-31 wave is still 3% of the first. Give the jump a and the bars follow 1/n for a while, then bend down onto a line as steep as 1/n^2. The wider the ramp, the earlier they bend. At the , speed 31 is down to 0.1%.

This is a general rule. A jump costs sizes that fall as 1/n, a corner costs 1/n^2, and the smoother a wave is, the faster its spectrum falls away. Fine detail in time means a wide spread in speed.

Sound

Sound is the everyday place where all of this is real. A sound is air pressure going up and down at your ear, and a steady note is a pressure wave that repeats a few hundred times a second. Its repeat rate sets the pitch: the standard tuning note, the A above middle C, repeats 440 times a second. Its spectrum sets everything else about how it sounds.

A guitar string shows where a spectrum comes from. Pulled aside at one point and let go, it starts as two straight lines meeting at a corner. That shape, like any shape of a string pinned at both ends, is a sum of the string's own modes: half a sine wave between the ends, a whole one, one and a half, and so on, each vibrating at its own whole-number multiple of the lowest pitch. Plucked , the string holds every mode except the 5th and 10th. Those modes have a still point, a node, exactly where the finger was, so they can't help make a shape whose biggest bend is there. Pluck and all the even modes vanish, since each has a node at the center. Pluck , the way a sharp, bright guitar sound is made, and the higher modes grow.

Each mode vibrates at its own whole-number multiple of the lowest frequency, so the sound the string makes is a repeating wave whose spectrum is these bars. I've computed the motion as nothing more than the sum of the modes, each swinging at its own speed. The corner that runs back and forth along the string is what that sum looks like .

Your inner ear does something close to this measurement: different spots along the cochlea respond to different frequencies, so the ear hears a sound partly as its spectrum.

Arrows that turn

The pair of numbers from the phase figure, a sine part and a cosine part that together act like one arrow, is a hint. Mathematicians have a number system in which arrows are the numbers: the complex numbers. Each one is a point in a plane, or the arrow from the center to it. The arrow one step right is 1; the arrow one step up is called i. Adding two complex numbers puts the arrows tip to tail, as with the circles. Multiplying them is where it gets useful. Drag the tips of the blue and orange arrows and watch their product:

The product always points at the sum of the two angles, and its length is the product of the two lengths. So multiplying by an arrow of length 1 does nothing but turn. Multiplying by , which points straight up, turns any arrow a quarter turn counterclockwise; doing it twice turns it half a turn, which is why i \times i = -1. Multiplying by turns it half a turn. Multiplication is turning.

Now take the arrow of length 1 at angle \theta. Its tip is at \cos\theta across and \sin\theta up, so it's the complex number \cos\theta + i\sin\theta. This arrow has a famous short name, e^{i\theta}, for reasons in the note below, and the only thing to know about it here is that it's a turn by \theta. Let \theta grow with time and the arrow spins. Turn the view to see the spinning tip spread out along time:

Looked at , it's the circle from the start of this page. From , the height of the tip draws \sin t. From , its sideways position draws \cos t, in green. One spinning arrow, e^{it} = \cos t + i \sin t, carries both waves.Why the exponential? Grow \theta a little and e^{i\theta} gets multiplied by e^{i\,d\theta} \approx 1 + i\,d\theta, a tiny step at right angles to the arrow. Steps always at right angles to the arrow keep its length fixed and walk its tip round a circle. That's Euler's formula, e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta. A dot at speed k on a circle of size c is then just c\,e^{ikt}, and its starting angle can be folded into c by letting c be an arrow too.

That makes negative speeds easy to say: e^{-it} spins the other way, clockwise. And a plain up-and-down wave turns out to be two arrows spinning in opposite directions. Here an arrow spinning counterclockwise carries a second one spinning clockwise at the same speed. Change the second arrow's length, and watch the path of the tip:

With the arrows , whatever one does upward the other does downward, and the tip just slides back and forth along the line: the plain wave \cos t = \tfrac12 e^{it} + \tfrac12 e^{-it}. With the tip leaves the line and goes round an ellipse. So the sine and cosine waves of the earlier sections were each a matched pair of circles, one per direction, and the two numbers measured for each speed are the arrow describing that pair.

Drawing with circles

Back to the note at the top. A drawing isn't a wave going up and down; it's a pen moving around a plane. But a point in a plane is a complex number, so a drawing traced once around, over one turn of time, is a repeating complex wave. Its pieces are single arrows c\,e^{ikt}, and nothing forces them into matched pairs any more. Speeds can be positive or negative, and each one stands alone.

Here is the smallest example: a big circle turning counterclockwise at speed 1, with a smaller one on its rim. Step the small circle's speed through negative and positive values and watch the closed path the pen draws:

To find the circles hidden in a drawing, the multiply-and-average trick works almost unchanged. Multiplying by e^{-ikt} turns each point back by kt, so it unspins the drawing at speed k. The circle that was turning at exactly that speed now stands still, while every other circle still turns a whole number of times and averages away. Keeping the biggest

That last figure is the idea behind most of the compression in your pocket. Describe a signal by its spectrum, keep the big amounts, throw away the small ones, and store far fewer numbers for nearly the same result. Real audio formats like MP3 and AAC cut sound into short overlapping windows like this one, describe each with a close cousin of these waves, and then spend their bits by a model of hearing: amounts the ear can't notice, often because a louder sound at a nearby frequency drowns them out, are stored coarsely or not at all .

Most signals people care about are smooth most of the time, so their spectra are dominated by a few large amounts. Storing those and dropping the rest is the core of how sound and image files are made small.

Circles everywhere

Every figure on this page did the same two things. It found circles by multiplying and averaging, and it rebuilt something by adding them back up. Computers do the finding with the fast Fourier transform, which gets every amount for N points in about N \log_2 N steps instead of N^2. For a million points that's about twenty million steps instead of a trillion. James Cooley and John Tukey published it in 1965 , and it turned out Gauss had used the same trick around 1805, in work published only after his death .

The idea reaches well past sound and pictures. Fourier invented it to follow heat, because each circle's worth of temperature fades at its own steady rate, so the hard problem splits into many easy ones . The same split handles vibrating drums, quantum particles, and tide tables. And not every signal repeats: a single clap or one heartbeat doesn't. Let the repeat get longer and longer and the whole-number speeds crowd together until they fill in every speed between them, which is the subject of the last note below.

From repeating waves to any wave: the Fourier transform 3 min

A single blip that never repeats can be treated as a repeating wave with a very long repeat time T. The whole-number speeds of a wave that repeats every T seconds are the frequencies 1/T, 2/T, 3/T, \ldots, so as T grows they sit closer and closer together. Lengthen the gap between blips and watch the spectrum:

Measured at each allowed frequency and scaled by T, the amounts always land on the same curve. As T grows, the stems fill it in completely, and in the limit a single blip has an amount at every frequency, not just at whole multiples. The sum over speeds becomes an integral over frequency, and the multiply-and-average becomes

\hat f(\nu) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\, e^{-2\pi i \nu t}\, dt

the Fourier transform: unspin the signal at frequency \nu and add up what's left. Going back is the same with the sign of the spin flipped, f(t) = \int \hat f(\nu)\, e^{2\pi i \nu t}\, d\nu. The dashed curve here is \sin(\pi\nu)/\pi\nu, the transform of a one-second blip. Its first gap at 1 per second, and the way it spreads wider for shorter blips, is the same trade-off as before: the sharper a signal is in time, the more spread out it is in frequency .

A pen at the end of a chain of turning circles turned out to be enough to copy a note, a fish, or a scribble. Every repeating wave and every closed drawing is a recipe of circles, and the recipe can be read off the shape by multiplying and averaging. The only places it struggles are the sudden jumps, where the 9% overshoot never goes away.
