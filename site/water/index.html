Why water is so strange

What makes everyday water behave the way it does, from one bent molecule to the ripples on a pond.

  • A water molecule is bent, with its charge pushed to one side, so neighboring molecules stick to each other through hydrogen bonds that form and break about once a picosecond.
  • Those bonds make water boil 150 °C later than its chemical cousins would suggest, soak up four to ten times more heat than rock or metal, and reach its densest at 4 °C, which is why lakes keep liquid water under their ice.
  • At the surface the same bonds pull inward as surface tension, strong enough to float a steel paperclip and to lift water up a thin tube; around a dissolved ion they line up as a shell, which is why water dissolves salt.
  • In motion, water is thin: small things in slow water flow smoothly, but almost any stream is turbulent, and its waves travel at speeds set by gravity, depth, and, for the smallest ripples, surface tension.
  • Water bends light by a third, focusing sunlight into the bright web on a pool floor, and it absorbs red light weakly but steadily, so a few meters of it look blue.

A glass of tap water is about the least remarkable thing in a kitchen: no color, no smell, no taste. Yet almost every number that describes it is odd. A molecule this small should be a gas at room temperature. Water takes more heat to warm than nearly any other liquid, swells when it freezes, climbs narrow tubes, holds up insects, and dissolves more substances than any other common liquid.

Three things happen that shouldn't. Warming from 20 °C to 100 °C the water swells by about 4%, normal for a liquid, but cooling it toward freezing doesn't shrink it all the way: below 4 °C it starts to swell again. Freezing makes it jump up by 9%, the opposite of almost every other substance. And it stays liquid until 100 °C, far hotter than a molecule this light has any right to.

Each of these oddities comes from the shape of one molecule and the way it holds its neighbors, so that is where we start. The crystal of ice has its own page; this one follows water as a liquid, out to the waves on a pond.

A bent molecule

A water molecule is one oxygen atom holding two hydrogen atoms. Spectroscopy pins its shape down very precisely: each O–H bond is 0.9572 Å long, a little under a tenth of a nanometer, and the two bonds meet at 104.52° . Turn the molecule, switch between the stick model and one where each atom fills the space it occupies, and show the charges:

Oxygen pulls on the electrons it shares much harder than hydrogen does, so they spend more of their time near the oxygen. That leaves the oxygen end slightly negative, δ−, and each hydrogen slightly positive, δ+. Because the molecule is bent, the charges sit on opposite sides. The purple arrow, drawn from the negative end toward the positive end, is the dipole, 1.85 debye for a single water molecule .A straight molecule like carbon dioxide has no dipole at all: its two bonds point in opposite directions and cancel.

The bend comes from the oxygen's four pairs of outer electrons: two shared with the hydrogens, two kept as lone pairs on the far side. The four pairs push apart toward the corners of a tetrahedron, squeezed a little by the lone pairs to 104.5° instead of 109.5°. The ice page lets you bend the molecule and watch the dipole change. Here I want to show what a dipole does in bulk.

Only around a hundred million volts per meter, a hundred times the field that makes air spark, does the lineup become easy to see. A fraction of a percent sounds like nothing, but a liter holds 3.3 × 1025 molecules, each leaning its negative end toward the positive plate. Together they cancel most of the plates' field: in water what remains is 78 times weaker than in empty space . We'll need it to dissolve salt.

Hydrogen bonds

Opposite charges attract, so the positive hydrogen of one molecule is drawn toward the negative oxygen of the next. When a hydrogen points straight at a neighbor's oxygen, the pair holds together with about 20 kilojoules per mole, roughly a twentieth of a real chemical bond but eight times the energy of random thermal jostling at room temperature. This link is the hydrogen bond, and it only works when the molecules line up.

To watch many at once I'll use a flattened water, a well-studied model in which each molecule is a disc with three arms that bond only when two arms point straight at each other Real water has four bonding directions in three dimensions, two hydrogens and two lone pairs; three arms is what fits on a flat page.. The molecules move by Newton's laws with real masses and bond energies, jostled at the temperature you set. Blue dashes are hydrogen bonds. Heat the liquid from and watch the network come apart:

At room temperature about five arms in six are bonded, in a tangled net of rings. Yet the net is never still. Watch one dash: it breaks as a molecule swings an arm away, and a moment later the arm finds another partner. The liquid flows because bonds are swapped one at a time, not because they are all broken. At , liquid only under pressure, many more arms hang free and the net frays into clusters.

Real water behaves the same way, with four bonding directions instead of three. At room temperature each molecule has, on average, between three and four hydrogen bonds at any instant.Ice, with exactly four, is only about 15% more bonded than the liquid: melting breaks surprisingly few bonds.

How long does one bond last? Here is the same liquid, with one molecule ringed in orange and its bonds drawn in orange. Every time a bond breaks anywhere in the box, the simulation adds its age to the histogram on the right:

Most bonds last less than a picosecond, a trillionth of a second, and a few last several. In the model the average is close to 0.8 ps at room temperature; ultrafast infrared measurements on real water find the network loses its memory of where its bonds were in about 1.2 ps . A molecule swaps a partner by a sudden jump, turning an arm from one neighbor to the next in one quick motion . Warm the liquid and the jumps come faster.

So liquid water is a crowd holding hands, each person letting go and grabbing a new hand a trillion times a second, while staying in contact with nearly everyone around them.

How the flat-water simulation works 2 min

Each molecule is a disc with three arms 120° apart. Two molecules repel when they overlap and attract weakly at a distance (a Lennard-Jones pull, a tenth of a bond's strength), and they form a hydrogen bond when they are about 2.8 Å apart and an arm of each points along the line between them. The bond's energy falls off as a narrow bell curve in the distance and in each arm's angle, which is the Mercedes-Benz model of Silverstein, Haymet and Dill .

I set its bond energy to 20 kJ/mol, its mass to a water molecule's, and its bond length to 2.8 Å, which fixes the time scale: one model time unit is 0.27 ps. The molecules move by Newton's laws, with a thermostat (Langevin dynamics) standing in for the rest of the liquid: a little friction and random kicks sized to the temperature. A bond counts as formed when the pair's energy drops below half a bond and as broken when it rises above a fifth, so a bond that only wobbles isn't counted as breaking. The model is two-dimensional and has three arms instead of four, so its numbers are a guide to the real liquid, not a measurement of it.

Water's molecules are bent and lopsided in charge, so each one holds three or four neighbors by hydrogen bonds. The bonds are swapped about once a picosecond, but at any moment nearly all of them are in place.

Late to boil

The boiling point measures how hard a liquid holds its molecules. Normally that grip grows with size: bigger molecules have more electrons, which slosh into brief lopsided charges that pull on neighbors. So within one column of the periodic table, compounds of hydrogen boil later the heavier they get. Below are the measured boiling points of four such families . The dashed line through hydrogen sulfide, selenide, and telluride reaches about −90 °C at water's row. Instead it boils 190 °C later; by the trend, there would be no oceans. Turn on the and the pattern is clear: methane and its cousins, which form no hydrogen bonds, rise steadily with size and have no jump at the start. Ammonia and hydrogen fluoride, which also form hydrogen bonds, stick out the same way, but less.

Water sticks out furthest because each molecule makes four hydrogen bonds, two through its hydrogens and two through its lone pairsAmmonia has only one lone pair to accept a bond with; hydrogen fluoride has only one hydrogen to give.. To boil, a molecule has to break all of them at once, which takes 41 kJ per mole at 100 °C, among the largest costs of any liquid. It is why sweat cools you: each gram that evaporates carries 2.4 kJ away from your skin.

Slow to warm

The network costs energy to warm as well as to break.

A kilogram of water and a kilogram of iron get the same amount of heat. Which warms more?

Each gram of iron needs 0.45 J per degree, each gram of water 4.18 J, so the same heat warms iron about nine times more.

Put one kilogram each of water, dry sand, and iron on identical 500 W heaters and add the same heat to each, from :

After 120 kJ, four minutes on the heater, the iron is near 290 °C, the sand past 180 °C, and the water at 49 °C. Each gram of water needs 4.18 joules to warm by one degree, five to six times what sand needs and nine times what iron needs . In water much of the heat goes into the network: bending, stretching, and breaking hydrogen bonds, which soaks up energy without making the molecules move faster.

The comparison is fairest per gram. Switch to and iron, seven times denser, catches up: per liter it holds 85% as much heat per degree as water does. Water's real advantage is that it is light, cheap, liquid, and everywhere. That is why the oceans hold more heat in their top few meters than the whole atmosphere above them, why coastal towns have milder seasons than inland ones, and why car engines and power stations are cooled with water.

Densest at 4 °C

The network also explains the dip in the first figure. Most liquids are densest just above freezing: cool them and their molecules jiggle less and settle closer together. Water does that too, but its hydrogen bonds hold molecules at fixed angles that leave gaps, the open arrangement that becomes ice. The curve peaks at 3.98 °C, at 999.97 kg/m³. Above that, the usual expansion with warmth wins; below it, more of the water locks into open, ice-like patches and swells. A difference of 0.013% between 4 °C and 0 °C sounds negligible. In a lake it decides everything, because the densest water sinks.

Here is a lake 20 meters deep in a temperate climate, its temperature computed through a year from the air above it, the sun, wind mixing near the surface, and one rule: water denser than the water below it sinks. Scrub through the year from and watch the profile on the right:

In the sun warms the top few meters, and the lighter warm water floats on the cold, with a sharp step between them. In the surface cools, sinks, and stirs the lake until all of it nears 4 °C. Then something different happens. Water cooled below 4 °C is lighter than the 4 °C water beneath it, so it stays on top, cools further, and freezes there. In the lake is ice over a thin layer near 0 °C over a deep body at 4 °C, and in spring the order flips again.

Ice floating on top insulates the water beneath it, so the lake freezes slowly from the top and, in all but the shallowest ponds, never to the bottom. Fish spend the winter in the 4 °C water at the bottom. This model is simple, with one dimension and no inflowing rivers, but its shape, two turnovers a year, matches what is measured in real temperate lakes .

A skin on the surface

A molecule deep in the liquid is pulled equally from every side. One at the surface has bonding partners only below and beside it. Here is the flat water again, now as a slab with air above, its molecules shaded by how many bonds they hold, from pale for none to dark for all three. The plot on the right averages the bonds per molecule at each height as the simulation runs:

In the middle of the slab the molecules hold as many bonds as anywhere. In the top layer they hold fewer: there is no one above to bond with. Each missing bond is energy the liquid would rather not spend, so water pulls molecules inward and keeps its surface as small as it canThe bottom layer, against the floor of the box, loses bonds too. How many a real surface takes away decides whether a drop on it spreads or beads up.. That pull is surface tension. For water at 25 °C it is 0.0720 newtons per meter, or 72 millijoules per square meter of new surface , more than any common liquid but mercury.

Surface tension makes a small drop round, and gravity flattens a big one. Which one wins depends on size. Grow a drop from and change the surface it sits on:

A drop of a microliter or less is a near-perfect slice of a sphere, whatever it sits on. Grow it past about and its top flattens; by a few milliliters it is a puddle whose height no longer grows at all, about 4.5 mm on wax. The crossover is set by the capillary length, the square root of surface tension over density times gravity, 2.7 mm for water . Smaller than that, surface tension wins; bigger, gravity does.

The angle where the drop meets the surface, its contact angle, depends on how much the surface attracts water compared with water attracting itself. Glass, covered in oxygen atoms water can bond to, is wetted: the drop spreads. Wax offers nothing to bond with, and the drop beads at about 110°. A adds a forest of waxy bumps that trap air, so the drop sits mostly on air and rolls off.

The same pull can hold up something that should sink. A paperclip is made of steel, nearly eight times as dense as water, yet laid gently on the surface it floats. Here is the wire in cross-section, pressing a dimple into the surface. Thicken the wire from until it sinks:

A paperclip wire about 0.9 mm across weighs 0.5 millinewtons per centimeter of length, and the surface can push back with up to about 1.8, so it floats with room to spare, resting in a dimple about three quarters of a millimeter deep. The orange arrows are the surface pulling along each side, 0.072 N per meter of wire; the steeper the dimple, the more of that pull points up. Past about no dimple is steep enough, and the wire breaks through .

The calculation says why the trick only works for small things. Weight grows with the wire's cross-section, the square of its width; the pull grows only with its length. Shrink anything and surface tension gains on gravity, which is why a pond skater can stand on water and a person can't.

Two clean glass tubes stand in water, one twice as wide as the other. How do the heights the water climbs compare?

The pull along the rim grows with the tube's width, but the weight of the column it must hold grows with the width squared, so halving the width doubles the height.

Where water meets a surface it likes, the edge climbs. In a narrow glass tube the climbing edges meet and lift a whole column. Narrow the tube from and compare with :

Halve the radius and the water climbs twice as high: 15 mm in a tube 1 mm across, 30 cm in one a tenth of a millimeter across. The surface tension pulls up along the whole rim of the meniscus, which grows with the radius, while the water it has to hold grows with the radius squared, so thinner tubes win. This is Jurin's law:

h = \frac{2\,\gamma\cos\theta}{\rho\, g\, \green{r}}

In a plastic straw the contact angle is past 90°, the cosine turns negative, and the water inside sits slightly below the outside level. Capillary rise is how a paper towel drinks up a spill and how water wicks through soil. It isn't how trees lift water a hundred meters: their vessels are tens of micrometers across, good for under a meter of rise, and the rest is pulled up by evaporation from the leaves.

Where Jurin's law comes from 1 min

The meniscus meets the glass all round the rim, a length 2πr, pulling with γ per meter of rim, of which the fraction cos θ points up. The column it holds is πr²h of water, weighing ρgπr²h. Setting the two equal:

2\pi r\,\gamma\cos\theta = \rho g\,\pi r^2 h \quad\Rightarrow\quad h = \frac{2\gamma\cos\theta}{\rho g r}

With γ = 0.0727 N/m at 20 °C and a fully wetting tube (θ = 0), h × r = 14.8 mm², so a tube 1 mm across (r = 0.5 mm) lifts water 30 mm. The same balance, with the capillary length in place of the tube radius, sets how high a puddle can stand and how heavy a floating wire can be.

The great solvent

A grain of table salt vanishes in water within a minute; in oil it would sit forever. Salt is a crystal of sodium ions, Na+, and chloride ions, Cl−, held by the pull between opposite charges. Here is one ion among flat water molecules, now with real charges and shapes, every atom pulling on every other. Switch between and and watch which end of each nearby molecule faces the ion:

Around sodium, the waters touching it all turn their negative oxygen ends inward and their hydrogens out, at 2.4 Å from the ion's center. Around chloride, larger and negative, each touching water points one hydrogen at it instead, a little further out. The ringed molecules are the hydration shell. In real, three-dimensional water, X-ray and neutron measurements find five or six waters around a sodium ion and about six around chloride ; on a flat page only four or five fit.

The shell is how water pays for pulling the ion out of its crystal. Each turned molecule gives back some of the energy the ion lost by leaving its oppositely charged neighbors, and the shells keep the freed ions from finding each other again.

That second job is where the dipoles come back. Pull a sodium and a chloride ion apart, from touching at 2.8 Å to , and compare the energy it costs in empty space with the energy it costs in water:

In empty space the pair at contact is bound by about 500 kJ/mol, two hundred times the 2.5 kJ/mol of thermal jostling: the ions will never part by chance. In water the turned dipoles weaken the pull 78 times, so its curve hugs zero. : at contact it is down to about 6 kJ/mol, and by 7 Å, two layers of water apart, it is no bigger than the jostling itself. The ions wander off independently.Right at contact the water between the ions isn't a smooth medium and the factor of 78 is only a guide; the conclusion survives the full calculation.

The same holds for anything charged or polar: sugars, acids, the salts in blood and sea water, the charged parts of proteins. A liquid that weakens electric attraction 78 times and wraps every charge in a shell dissolves more substances than any other common liquid.Oil and wax, with no charge for water to grab, stay out: the other half of how soap and cell membranes work.

Flow

For all its stickiness between molecules, water pours easily. The resistance a liquid puts up to flowing, its viscosity, is set by how fast its molecules can trade places, and water's trade every picosecond. Warm it from and watch both its viscosity and the speed at which a fine grain of sand, a tenth of a millimeter across, sinks through it:

At 20 °C water's viscosity is 1.00 millipascal-second , thousands of times less than honey's. Heating to 100 °C cuts it by more than three times, because the bonds that hold molecules in place break faster, and the grain sinks three times faster to match. Cold water is noticeably thicker: near freezing its viscosity is 1.8 mPa·s, which is why sediment settles more slowly in a cold lake.

Water is 55 times as viscous as air, but it is also 830 times as dense, so it takes much more to stop it once it moves. Divide one by the other and water comes out 15 times "thinner" than air: a stirred cup of water keeps swirling far longer than a stirred cup of air would.

That is why flowing water is almost always turbulent. What decides it is one number, the Reynolds number: the speed of the flow times the width of the pipe, divided by the viscosity over the density. Below about 2,300 water slides down a pipe in smooth layers; above about 4,000 it tumbles . Raise it from and watch the speed across the pipe:

At low Reynolds number every layer slides over the next and the speed across the pipe is a parabola, fastest in the middle, still at the wall. Past about 2,300 a disturbance can grow into eddies; above 4,000 they always do, and the eddies carry fast water toward the walls, flattening the profile. A straw you sip through is near the line; the water in a kitchen tap is past it, at about 15,000, and a river is in the millions.

Osborne Reynolds found the rule in 1883 by letting a thin thread of dye into water flowing down a glass tube. At low speed the thread stayed a straight line the length of the tube; past a certain speed it suddenly broke up and colored the whole tube. The switch came at the same value of speed times width over viscosity, whatever the tube .

Because water's kinematic viscosity is so small, a pipe of water has to be very narrow or very slow to stay smooth: a centimeter-wide pipe goes turbulent at just 23 cm/s.

Waves

Drop a pebble in a pond and a ring spreads out, but not as one ring: it breaks into a widening train of ripples. Here is a shallow ripple tank a few millimeters deep, its surface computed from the wave equation. The pebble has just landed. It's slowed to a quarter of real speed, and drops a new pebble every few seconds. Compare the rings and :

Without surface tension, the waves in a shallow tray all travel at one speed, √(gh), and the ring stays a single neat band. With it, the short ripples run ahead of the long ones, so the disturbance sorts itself by wavelength: the shortest ripples in front, the longest at the back. Waves whose speed depends on their wavelength are called dispersive, and on deep water both kinds of wave are.

Two forces pull a bumped surface flat. Gravity pulls the crests down, and surface tension pulls the curved surface straight. For long waves gravity wins and longer is faster; for short ripples surface tension wins and shorter is faster. Drag the wavelength from and change the depth:

Every wave on water lies on the blue curve. It has a floor: no wave on clean water can move slower than 23 cm/s, which happens at a wavelength of 1.7 cm . Shorter than that, the ripples are ruled by surface tension and get faster as they shrink. Longer, they are ruled by gravity, and in deep water their speed grows with the square root of the wavelength: four times as long, twice as fast. That is why swell from a distant storm reaches the beach before its chop.

Once the wavelength is many times the depth, the bottom gets in the way and the speed stops growing at √(gh), whatever the wavelength. A tsunami, hundreds of kilometers long, is a shallow-water wave even over the 4 km deep ocean, and it crosses it at , the speed of a jet airliner.

Long water waves are pulled flat by gravity and short ones by surface tension. The two meet at 1.7 cm, where waves are slowest; over shallow water every long wave slows to √(gh).

The formula behind the curve 2 min

For small waves of wavelength λ on water of depth h, gravity g, density ρ and surface tension γ, the speed is :

c^2 = \left(\frac{g\lambda}{2\pi} + \frac{2\pi\gamma}{\rho\lambda}\right)\tanh\frac{2\pi h}{\lambda}

The first term in the bracket is gravity's, growing with wavelength; the second is surface tension's, growing as the wavelength shrinks. They are equal at λ = 2π√(γ/ρg), 1.7 cm, where the speed is lowest. The tanh is close to 1 in deep water; when the depth is small compared with the wavelength it becomes 2πh/λ, and the gravity term gives c² = gh. The figure plots this formula with water's values at 20 °C. It holds for waves whose height is small compared with their length; steep waves run a little faster and, near breaking, the formula no longer applies.

Waves bend around obstacles. Send straight waves at a harbor wall with one opening, and widen the gap from , comparing it with the wavelength of 6 cm:

Through a they pass mostly straight, as a beam that spreads only at its edges. The rule is the same for light and sound: an opening bends waves strongly when it is about as wide as they are long. This is diffraction, the reason ripples reach the corners behind a harbor wall.

Where two sets of waves cross, their heights add. Here are two dippers bobbing in step; move them apart from and watch the pattern where their rings overlap:

Wherever a crest from one source meets a trough from the other, they cancel, and the water along that line barely moves. Where crests meet crests, the waves stand twice as tall. The still lines fan out from between the dippers, and each extra wavelength of separation adds another pair. This is interference, and the same pattern, made with light through two slits, is how Thomas Young argued in the early 1800s that light is a wave.

Waves rolling onto a beach feel the bottom long before they break. Here is a wave train arriving from deep water over a sloping bed; set the period, the time between crests, from :

Out where the water is deep compared with the wavelength, the crests run at their deep-water speed. As the bed rises they slow toward √(gh): 10 m/s over 10 m of water, 3 m/s over one meter. The crests behind are still fast, so they crowd together; the wavelength shrinks, and the energy squeezed into each shortening wave makes it grow taller until, at a height about four fifths of the depth, its crest outruns its base and it breaks. The same slowing swings waves round to face a beach, whatever direction they came from.

Water and light

Light slows down in water to three quarters of its speed in air, which is what a refractive index of 1.333 means , and it bends wherever it crosses the surface at a slant. Look at a coin on the bottom of a pool 2 m deep and drag the eye to look from further off to the side:

Your eye assumes light travels in straight lines, so it traces the bent ray straight back and puts the coin where that line ends. Looking straight down, the coin seems 1.5 m deep, three quarters of the real depth, the same factor as the slowing. Looking from the side, the ghost rises further: at 60° from straight down the coin seems only about 1 m deep. Every pool looks shallower than it is, and the far end most of all.

As the water rises, the floor seen through it lifts and the lane line kinks where the water's edge crosses it. The tiles also waver, because every ripple on the surface is a small slanted pane that bends the light a slightly different way, and the deeper the water, the bluer the floor looks. Those same ripples do something stranger to the light reaching the floor.

Each crest of a wave is a weak lens. It bends the sunlight passing through it toward a line below, and where those lines land the floor is brighter; between them it's dimmer. By about a meter deep, each crest's light comes to a sharp line on the floor, and the lines join into the shifting bright web called caustics. In light from several crests crosses on the way down and the web tangles into a finer, busier net. The same focusing makes the dancing bright patterns on the hull of a boat and the underside of a bridge.

Water isn't blue only because it reflects the sky. A white tile at the bottom of an indoor pool, lit by white lamps, still looks blue: the light reaching your eye has lost its red to the water itself.

The deeper water also turned blue. Water absorbs a little of every color, but very unevenly: red light is absorbed about a hundred times more strongly than blue . Here is the measured absorption, and the color of a white tile seen through water as you sink it from :

Water's absorption bottoms out in the blue near 420 nm and climbs a hundredfold into the red. A glass of water is far too thin for this to show. Through a few meters, light from a white tile comes back short of red and looks pale cyan; by 10 m only blue and some green are left. It isn't scattering, as in the sky, nor a dissolved dye: water's own molecules absorb red light by setting their O–H bonds vibrating, a faint echo of their strong absorption in the infrared . Back to the glass

Back to the glass of water from the start, where we can now name what it does. Pick an oddity below the glass; each sets the temperature where it shows:

Nearly every item traces back to two facts: the molecule is bent, and its charge sits lopsided across the bend. That gives each molecule four places to hold a neighbor, and liquid water is those holds made and broken a trillion times a second. The heat it soaks up, its boiling point, the 4 °C bottom of a winter lake, the skin that floats a paperclip, the shells around salt: each is the network's price or its pull. The waves and the light are more general physics, but their numbers, the 1.7 cm ripple, the blue, are water's own.

Plenty is still argued about. Whether supercooled water, below about −40 °C, hides a second critical point between two liquids of different density is an open question , and so is exactly how the network rearranges in the instant a bond breaks.
