• Air is a crowd of molecules, 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen, each flying at about 500 m/s and hitting a neighbor seven billion times a second.
  • Pressure is the drumming of those hits on every surface. At sea level it adds up to 101 kPa, the weight of ten tonnes on each square meter, pressing from every side at once.
  • The weight of the air above sets the pressure, so it halves about every 5.5 km you climb. Warm air is thinner than cool air at the same pressure, so it floats up.
  • Wind is air sliding from high pressure toward low, turned sideways by the Earth's spin until it circles around storms instead of filling them.
  • Air flowing past a body curves, and curving air needs a difference in pressure to bend it. That difference is drag behind a cylinder and lift under a wing.

Blow out a candle and a thread of smoke climbs from the wick, wavers, and tears itself into curls. Stand in a dim room with the sun coming through a gap in the curtains and the beam fills with specks of dust that drift and turn, though nothing in the room is moving. Both are showing you something you can't see: the air, which pushes the smoke up and carries the dust around.

Air is easy to forget because it is so thin and so clear, yet a cubic meter of it weighs 1.2 kg. Everything in this article, from why your ears pop to why a plane stays up, comes from asking what that matter is doing. Drag across the room to wave your hand through the air, and watch the dust:

The dust doesn't fall the way a pebble would. The smallest motes take minutes to sink a meter, and each one swerves wherever the air around it goes, so the swirl you left behind keeps stirring them long after your hand has gone. The dust is tracing the motion of something far heavier than itself in total: the 50 kg or so of air in a small room.

The candle shows the same stuff from another side. Turn the candle, then press Blow out:

Your breath is air you've set moving, and moving air pushes: hard enough to bend the flame over and carry its heat away faster than it is made, so it dies. Then the warm smoke climbs, though nothing pulls it up. Air pushed on the flame, and air lifted the smoke. To see how, we have to look at what air is made of.

Molecules

If you could magnify a speck of air a hundred million times, you would see separate molecules flying in straight lines and bouncing off each other. Most are nitrogen, two nitrogen atoms bound together; about a fifth are oxygen, also in pairs; and one molecule in a hundred is a lone atom of argon . Here are 220 of them in those proportions, in a glass box you can turn. Follow one molecule, and change the temperature :

Each molecule runs straight until it meets another, then both fly off in new directions, tumbling as they go. The followed molecule's trail is a zigzag of straight lines, and no two legs are the same length. Heat the gas and every molecule speeds up, but nothing else about the picture changes. Temperature, at the level of molecules, is how fast they move.

The speeds in the status line are real. At room temperature a nitrogen molecule averages 471 m/s, faster than a rifle bullet, and oxygen, a little heavier, 440 m/s. But no two molecules move at the same speed, and the spread has a definite shape. Give every molecule exactly the same speed and watch the histogram of speeds:

One tall bar falls apart into a lopsided hump within a few collisions per molecule. Each collision hands energy from one molecule to the other at random, and after enough trades the speeds settle into the one spread that further trading doesn't change. James Clerk Maxwell worked out its shape in 1860 . From any start, the bars end on the curve.

Here is Maxwell's distribution for each of the three gases of air on its own. Drag the temperature and watch where the nitrogen and oxygen peaks move:

Heat the air and both peaks slide right and flatten. The average grows as the square root of the absolute temperature, so going from 20 °C to 600 °C, three times hotter in kelvin, raises it by only √3, about 70%. Temperature fixes the average energy, ½mv², so lighter molecules move faster: argon, 40 times as heavy as a hydrogen atom, is the slowest of the three.

The shaded tail matters more than it looks. Its molecules are the ones that break bonds, which is why chemical reactions speed up so steeply with temperature, and why light hydrogen leaks from the top of the atmosphere into space while nitrogen stays.

How far does a molecule fly between collisions? The box above exaggerated how crowded air is. Here is a cube of real air 20 nm on a side, with every molecule in it at its true size. Climb to and watch the distance a molecule flies before it hits another, its mean free path:

At sea level the molecules sit about 3.4 nm apart, ten times their own width, and a molecule flies about 66 nm, twenty spacings, before it happens to hit one . Air is mostly empty space. At 470 m/s that flight takes a seventh of a nanosecond, so each molecule collides about seven billion times a second. Climb, and the cube empties: at the mean free path is a few micrometers, and by 60 km it is a quarter of a millimeter.

Air is a thin crowd of molecules, each flying at about the speed of a bullet in a straight line for some 66 nm before it hits another. Everything air does is what that crowd does when you push on it, heat it, or let it fall.

Pressure

When a molecule bounces off a wall it reverses its motion toward the wall, and the wall has to supply the push that reverses it. By Newton's third law the molecule pushes back just as hard, for a moment. One molecule makes a tap. Many make a steady push. Set how many molecules there are and watch the force on the right-hand wall over time:

With the wall feels nothing most of the time and a sharp kick now and then. With the kicks overlap into a nearly steady push, and the swings shrink as one over the square root of the number: ten times the molecules, about a third the wobble. A square centimeter of wall in room air takes about 3×10²³ hits a second, so the push it feels is as smooth as anything in physics. That push per unit area is pressure.

Each hit delivers twice the molecule's momentum toward the wall, so pressure grows with three things: how many molecules hit, how often, and how hard. Squeeze the same molecules into half the space and each one returns to the wall twice as often. Heat them and they hit both more often and harder, each in proportion to their speed, so the push grows with speed squared, which is with temperature. Drag the piston to change the volume, and set the temperature:

Halve the volume and the measured pressure doubles; raise the temperature from 20 °C to 150 °C, from 293 K to 423 K, and it rises by 44%. The dot never strays far from the ring, though nothing in the simulation knows the formula. It only bounces molecules. The rule they obey is the ideal gas law:

P\,\green{V} = N k\,\orange{T}

Here N is the number of molecules and k is Boltzmann's constant, 1.380649×10⁻²³ J/K . The law treats molecules as points that never meet, which is why I switched off their collisions here; real air at room conditions departs from it by less than a tenth of a percent. With it, 2.5×10²⁵ molecules per cubic meter at 293 K make 101 kPa.

Take a box of side L and one molecule with velocity component vx toward a wall. Each hit transfers 2mvx, and it returns to the same wall every 2L/vx seconds, so its average force is mvx²/L. Adding N molecules and dividing by the wall's area L² gives P = Nm⟨vx²⟩/V. Temperature is defined so that each direction of motion carries ½kT of energy on average, ½m⟨vx²⟩ = ½kT, and the product PV = NkT drops out. Collisions between molecules don't change the answer: they shuffle momentum between molecules but don't create or destroy any.

101 kPa is 101,325 newtons on every square meter, the weight of 10.3 tonnes, or a kilogram's weight on each square centimeter of your skin. We don't notice because it pushes from every side at once and the pushes cancel. Remove the air from one side and they stop cancelling. Here are two steel bowls 30 cm across, pressed rim to rim. Pump out the air inside, down to of it:

Pump out and the force reaches 7.2 kN, the weight of a 730 kg car. The air outside presses on the bowls everywhere, and only where the inside air used to press back does the push go unanswered. In the 1650s Otto von Guericke, the mayor of Magdeburg, did this with larger bowls in public, and teams of horses failed to pull them apart. Once he let the air back in, they came apart by hand.

A vacuum doesn't suck. There is no pull from empty space; the bowls are pushed together by the air outside. A drinking straw works the same way: you lower the pressure in your mouth, and the atmosphere pushes the drink up the straw.

The weight of the sky

The bowls show that the air pushes, but not why it pushes this hard. The answer is that the air is heavy and stacked high. Every layer of air rests on the layer below it, so the air at the bottom carries the weight of everything above. That weight, spread over each square meter, is the pressure. Climb up through the column of air above one square meter, to :

The air above one square meter of sea weighs 10,330 kg, and the pressure at the bottom, 101 kPa, is that weight in newtons per square meter. Climb and you leave part of the load beneath you. At 5.5 km you have climbed past half the air and the pressure is half; at , where airliners cruise, three quarters of the air is below and the cabin has to be pumped up; on the summit of you stand above two thirds of it.

The falloff isn't a straight line. The pressure at each height squeezes the air there, and squeezed air is denser, so the bottom layers hold more air per meter than the top ones. Each kilometer of climbing therefore removes the same fraction of what's left, not the same amount, and the result is close to an exponential. For a column at one temperature it is exactly one, the barometric formula:

P(z) = P_0\, e^{-z/H}, \qquad H = \frac{RT}{Mg} \approx 8.4\ \mathrm{km}

The scale height H compares the energy of a molecule's motion, RT per mole, with the energy it takes to lift a mole of gas one meter, Mg. Warm the gas and the column stands taller; swap air for , seven times lighter, and the scale height grows to about 60 km. Heavy on its own would sit lower, with a scale height of 5.5 km. The wind keeps the real atmosphere stirred, so its gases don't separate this way below about 80 km. The real column is also colder higher up, down to −56 °C above 11 km, so its pressure falls a little faster than the dashed curve of the last figure.

Thin air changes what a body can do. Water boils when its vapor pressure matches the pressure of the air on it, so lower pressure means a lower boiling point. Each breath, too, takes in a fixed volume of air, so it carries fewer molecules of oxygen. Go to , , or the summit of , or set the altitude :

"There's less oxygen at altitude" is true, but not because the air changes its recipe. Up to about 80 km the air is mixed by the wind and stays 21% oxygen. There are fewer molecules of everything in each liter, oxygen included.

Warm air rises

The weight of the column also explains the smoke. Pressure grows with depth, so anything in air feels a bigger push on its bottom than on its top, and the difference is the weight of the air it shoves aside. That is buoyancy. Anything lighter than the air it displaces rises, and warm air is lighter: by the gas law, at the same pressure, air at 100 °C holds 293/373 as many molecules as air at 20 °C.

A hot-air balloon is a bag of that lighter air. This one holds 2,800 m³ and, with its basket, burners, fuel, and five people, weighs 750 kg. Set the air inside to and watch where it settles:

At 96 °C the hot air lifts exactly 750 kg and the balloon sits on the ground. Hotter, it climbs, but the air outside thins as it goes, and so does the difference in weight between outside and inside. At it levels off near 1.6 km; at , about as hot as the fabric can take, near 4.3 km. Let the air cool and it sinks. Pilots fly by doing exactly this, a few seconds of burner at a time.

A fire makes its own balloon of hot gas with no fabric around it. The flame heats the air and gases above it to several hundred degrees, making them a third as dense as the room, and they rise as a plume, carrying the soot we see as smoke. Turn the fire and set the wind to :

In still air the smoke goes straight up and widens as it climbs, dragging room air in at its edges. Each meter up it is cooler and its lift weaker, so smoke slows and eventually spreads in a flat layer. In a the plume bends over: it rises as before, but drifts sideways faster than it climbs. Outdoors, where warm air rises and cool air sinks, the air also has to move sideways to replace it.

Wind

Where air is warmed it rises and spreads out aloft, and the column over that spot loses weight; where air cools it sinks and the column gains. The result is highs and lows of pressure a few percent apart. Air near the ground is pushed from high toward low. Here is a map of the pressure as color, high and low. Drag the centers around, then release some air:

Every dot runs straight downhill on the pressure map, crossing the lines of equal pressure, the isobars, at right angles, and piles into the low. On a planet that didn't turn, wind would fill any low within hours. On Earth, winds blow almost along the isobars, round the lows, and storms last for days, because we watch the air from a spinning ball.

To see what spinning does, put a puck on a turntable. Launch it from the center and it slides off in a straight line, because nothing pushes it sideways. Spin the turntable at and watch the puck from the room, then from the turntable:

Seen from the room, the puck goes straight; the table turns under it, and the chalk mark it leaves on the surface curves. Seen from the turntable, the puck is the thing that curves, always to the right of the way it's moving when the table turns counterclockwise. Someone riding the table would say a sideways force pushed it. That apparent force is the Coriolis force, and it grows with the spin: and it vanishes.

The Earth is a turntable that turns once a day: counterclockwise seen from above the North Pole, so moving air there drifts to the right, and clockwise from above the South Pole, so air drifts left. The effect is strongest at the poles and zero on the equator. It is far too weak to steer the water in a sink, but air moving for hours across hundreds of kilometers feels it fully.

Now release a parcel of air at rest on a slope of pressure, with the low to the north. The pressure pushes it north, and as soon as it moves the Coriolis force pushes it to the right. Set the latitude to and follow it for two days:

The parcel never reaches the low. The faster it goes, the harder the Coriolis force pushes it sideways, until the two forces balance: the pressure force pulling toward the low and the Coriolis force pushing away from it, with the velocity at right angles to both, along the isobars. It overshoots that balance and loops around it, once every 17 hours at 45°, while drifting east. This balanced flow is the geostrophic wind :

V_g = \frac{1}{\rho f}\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta n}, \qquad f = 2\Omega \sin\varphi

Here Δp/Δn is how fast the pressure changes across the isobars, Ω is the Earth's spin, 7.29×10⁻⁵ radians per second, and φ the latitude. With 1 hPa per 100 km at 45° it gives 8 m/s, a fresh breeze. Closer to the the same slope drives a faster wind, because f is smaller. Near the ground, slows the air, the Coriolis force weakens with it, and the wind slants across the isobars toward the low, by 10° to 20° over the sea and more over land.

Put the balance around a low and the wind circles it. Drag the low around the globe, into the Southern Hemisphere and onto the equator:

North of the equator the wind runs counterclockwise around the low, south of it clockwise, and on the equator there is no circling, only air pouring in. That is why hurricanes almost never form within about 5° of the equator, and why those of the two hemispheres spin opposite ways. The circling keeps a low alive for days; only the layer slowed by friction leaks inward, rising at the center into cloud and rain. With the wind at your back in the Northern Hemisphere, the low is on your left.

Flowing around things

Wind meets trees, buildings, and wires and has to go around them, and how it goes around depends on one number. A flowing fluid carries momentum, which keeps each bit of it going straight, and it has viscosity, an internal friction that evens out speeds. Their ratio is the Reynolds number, Re = UD/ν: speed times size, divided by the kinematic viscosity, 1.5×10⁻⁵ m²/s for air . Around two eddies sit in the cylinder's shelter, turning in place, because the air sliding along the back can no longer climb against the rising pressure there and peels away, or separates. Past about 47 the pair can't stay put. The wake starts to sway, and eddies break off from the top and bottom by turns .

Real flows sit far to the right of this slider: a 2 cm twig in a 5 m/s breeze is at Re 7,000, a walking person at 50,000. Their wakes are turbulent, eddies of every size down to a fraction of a millimeter. Resolving them all would take billions of cells, so this simulation stays at the gentle end, where the first steps toward turbulence happen.

The pattern is a von Kármán vortex street, and its beat is set by the speed and the width of the body. Pairs leave at a frequency f with fD/U close to 0.2, a ratio called the Strouhal number. The simulation measures 0.18 at Re 150, and Roshko's wind-tunnel measurements gave 0.182 ; at higher Reynolds numbers it levels off near 0.2. Those pulls are why telephone wires sing in the wind. A wire 3 mm thick in a 10 m/s gust sheds eddies at 0.2 × 10 / 0.003, about 670 times a second, and the alternating push makes the wire hum at that pitch, the Aeolian tone; the sound explainer follows what the air does with it from there. The same pulls on a scale of meters can shake chimneys and bridges, so tall chimneys carry spiral fins to break up the rhythm.

A body in a stream also gets pushed downstream: drag. Most of it, for anything bigger than a grain of dust, comes from the low pressure in the wake. Every second the body shoves aside a volume of air proportional to its speed, and gives that air a velocity proportional to its speed, so the force grows as the speed squared. At 50 km/h the car's drag is 76 N; at 100 km/h it is 305 N, four times as much, and the power, force times speed, eight times. That cube is why fuel use climbs so fast on the motorway, and why a cyclist working twice as hard goes only a quarter faster. For the the parabola meets the weight at about 190 km/h: the terminal speed. The drag coefficient CD barely changes at these Reynolds numbers, which is what makes the v² rule hold.

Air flowing past a body has to curve, and curving air needs a difference in pressure to bend it. Behind a blunt body the curve fails, the flow separates, and the low pressure left in the wake drags the body back.

Lift

A wing is a body shaped so that the curving works for it instead of against it. Its rounded nose splits the stream gently, its long thin tail lets the flow leave smoothly from a sharp edge, and it meets the air tilted a few degrees nose up, its angle of attack. Here is a slice of a wing with tracers of air streaming past it. Turn it, and tilt it to :

Notice three things. The air does not split at the nose and rejoin at the tail as two equal streams: the dividing point sits under the nose, so more air goes over the top. The air over the top runs faster, its streaks paler and longer. And the whole stream leaves the trailing edge pointing down. The wing has turned the air downward, and by Newton's third law the air pushes the wing up.

Most people learned a different story at school: the top of the wing is longer, so the air over it must go faster to meet the air underneath at the trailing edge at the same time. Before you watch a line of smoke puffs go past the wing, decide:

Two puffs of smoke start side by side, one just above the stream that hits the wing's nose and one just below. Which reaches the trailing edge first?

Nothing makes the two halves of the stream meet again. The top air is pulled into the low pressure above the wing and arrives far ahead of its partner, which never catches up.

The puffs that go over the top arrive at the trailing edge long before their partners below, and the two halves of the line never meet again. The lower puff, which started nearer the dividing stream, crawls past the slow region under the nose and arrives later still. Babinsky filmed exactly this with real smoke in a wind tunnel . Raise the angle to and the lead grows, along with the lift.

Equal transit time is wrong: no law makes the air over a wing meet the air under it at the trailing edge, and it doesn't. A wing works because it bends the air, which needs low pressure on the outside of the bend. A flat sheet tilted to the wind lifts too, and planes fly upside down.

Air bends only when something pushes it sideways, and inside a fluid the only thing that can is a difference in pressure. Where air follows a curve, the pressure is lower on the inside of the curve than on the outside. Over a wing the stream curves down around the upper surface, so the pressure next to that surface must be below the pressure far away; under the wing the slowed, deflected air sits at higher pressure. Here is the pressure around a wing in a simulated stream, low and high. Tilt it to :

The low pressure over the top is larger and deeper than anything underneath, and the difference across the wing, pressing on its whole area, is the lift. The red spot at the nose is the stagnation point, where the oncoming air stops. Fast air and low pressure over the top are two views of one fact, Bernoulli's equation, p + ½ρv² = constant along a streamline: air speeds up as it moves into lower pressure. The curvature sets the pressure, and the pressure sets the speed.

So a steeper tilt bends the air more and makes more lift, but only up to a point. Lift is measured by the lift coefficient, the lift per square meter of wing in units of ½ρv². Raise the angle of attack to :

Up to about 10° the lift coefficient climbs in a straight line, about 0.1 for every degree, close to the 2π per radian (0.11 per degree) that thin-airfoil theory predicts . A cambered section, curved like this one, still lifts at 0° and only stops at about −2°. Then the line rounds over. At about the air over the top is asked to climb the steep rise in pressure toward the trailing edge, as it did behind the cylinder, and it can't: it separates, the low pressure on top collapses, and the lift falls. That is the stall.

The status line turns it into flying. Lift has to equal weight, ½ρv²SCL = mg, so a plane flies slowly only by flying at a high angle of attack, and the greatest lift coefficient sets the slowest it can fly. For a light airplane of 1,100 kg with 16 m² of wing, that is about 95 km/h. Flaps and slats, unfolded for takeoff and landing, change the wing's shape to push the peak higher.

Blue air

One more thing air does is visible every day: it is blue. Sunlight shakes each molecule's electrons, and the molecule re-sends a little of the light in other directions. For scatterers a thousand times smaller than a wavelength, that share grows as the inverse fourth power of the wavelength, so blue at 450 nm is scattered about five times as strongly as red at 700 nm . The blue of the sky is the air itself, lit by the sun.

The candle and the dust

Back to the dim room. Everything in it can now be named. Step through it:

The room is full of molecules flying at 500 m/s, hitting a mote of dust a hundredth of a millimeter across about 10¹⁸ times a second, from every side: the air's pressure, the weight of the whole column of air over the house. The dust is heavier than the air it displaces, so it sinks, but so slowly that the room's faint drafts carry it anywhere. The smoke is warm, so lighter than the air it pushes aside, and is lifted. It rises as a smooth thread, then, as its Reynolds number grows past a few hundred, tears into curls, the instability that shed eddies behind the cylinder.

The breath that blew out the flame was a pressure difference made by your lungs, pushing air out faster than the flame could hold its heat. And the beam is visible only because the motes scatter its light toward you; between them the molecules are scattering too, too weakly to see across a room but enough across tens of kilometers to turn the whole sky blue.

The same few ideas run from this room up to the weather. Warm air rising and cool air sinking make the highs and lows, the spinning Earth turns the wind around them, and every wing, sail, and feather works by curving the air. What no figure here can do is follow turbulence all the way down, eddies inside eddies to a millimeter. Predicting it in detail, for a wing, a storm, or a candle's smoke, is still one of the hard unsolved problems of physics.
