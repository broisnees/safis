• The heart is two pumps in one muscle. The right side pushes blood through the lungs, the left side through the rest of the body, and each beat they move the same 70 mL or so, because a pump that fills more pushes harder.
  • Four valves make each pump work. They have no muscles of their own: blood pressure opens them and shuts them, so they only ever let blood go one way.
  • An electrical wave times every beat. It starts in a few thousand pacemaker cells, waits a tenth of a second at the gate between atria and ventricles, then races through the ventricles in under a tenth of a second. An ECG is that wave, seen from the skin.
  • The heart feeds itself through its own small arteries. A heart attack is what happens when one of them is suddenly blocked: the muscle downstream starts dying within half an hour, from the inside out, and every minute it stays blocked costs more of it.

Press two fingers against the inside of your wrist, below the thumb, and wait. Each soft knock you feel is a wave of pressure that left your chest a fraction of a second earlier, when a fist-sized muscle squeezed about 70 milliliters of blood, a little under a third of a cup, into an artery already full of it. It will do this roughly a hundred thousand times today, two and a half billion times in a lifetime, and it never rests for longer than about half a second.

That muscle is two pumps in one body, with four one-way doors and its own electrical clock. This page takes it apart: how it fills and empties, what times each beat, and what goes wrong when the arteries that feed it clog. Here is a real-sized model, beating at a third of its resting speed. Turn it, and cut it open:

Inside are four hollow chambers: two thin-walled atria on top that collect blood, and two thick-walled ventricles below that push it out. The wall on the left of the picture, the patient's right, is thin. The wall on the patient's left is almost three times thicker. That difference is the first clue to how the whole thing works.

Two pumps

Blood makes two journeys, and each has its own pump. The right side of the heart sends it through the lungs, where it drops carbon dioxide and picks up oxygen, and back to the left side, which sends it out to everything else and back to the right. Change the heart rate and watch how much blood moves each minute:

At rest the heart moves about 5 liters a minute, close to all the blood in an adult body, so a red cell gets back to where it started about once a minute .

Because the loop is closed, the pumps are in series: every drop the right side sends into the lungs has to come out through the left. Over any stretch of minutes they must move exactly the same amount. How exactly?

Suppose the right ventricle pushed out just 1% more blood per beat than the left, 0.7 mL extra at a resting 75 beats a minute. How long before the lungs were in trouble?

0.7 mL × 75 beats is about 50 mL a minute, 3 liters an hour, piling up in lungs that normally hold about half a liter of blood. Within minutes the pressure in them would climb and fluid would start seeping into the air sacs.

Nothing wires the two pumps together, yet they match to a fraction of a percent. The trick is that each pump pushes harder when it is filled more. Model the circulation as two reservoirs, the body's veins and the lungs, with a pump moving blood from each to the other. Weaken the left pump to and watch the lungs:

For a few seconds the weak left pump falls behind, and blood comes into the lungs faster than it leaves. Their pressure rises, which fills the weak pump more, so it pushes harder, and within seconds the outputs match again, at a lower flow and a higher lung pressure. Nothing measured anything. This is the Frank–Starling law, named for Otto Frank, who saw it in frog hearts, and Ernest Starling, whose group measured it in dog hearts in 1914 .

Push it down to and the lung pressure needed to keep up passes about 20 mmHg, where fluid is forced out of the capillaries into the air sacs. That is the breathlessness of left heart failure, and it starts with nothing more than this arithmetic.

The two sides of the heart never measure each other. Each one simply pumps out what fills it, and that alone keeps them in step.

Four rooms, four doors

The real heart packs both pumps into one muscle, sharing a wall. Turn it and point at anything to name it; each name here lights its part. The superior and inferior vena cava bring blood from the body into the right atrium, then the right ventricle sends it up the pulmonary trunk to the lungs. The pulmonary veins bring it back to the left atrium, and the left ventricle pushes it out through the aorta:

It isn't laid out left and right as you might draw it. The right ventricle wraps around the front of the left one like a crescent, and the left ventricle makes the left side and the pointed tip, the apex, which points down, forward, and to the left. That is why you feel the beat just left of the breastbone. Now cut it open and compare the two ventricles' walls:

Both sides move the same 70 mL per beat, but not against the same resistance. The lungs are close and their vessels wide, so the right ventricle only reaches about 25 mmHg. The left ventricle has to reach about 120, nearly five times as much . Muscle grows to match its work, so the left wall is about a centimeter thick and the right only 3 to 5 mm.

The four valves are rings of tissue with two or three thin flaps, the leaflets. A valve has no muscle and no nerve. It opens when the blood behind it is at a higher pressure than the blood in front, and shuts the instant that reverses. Here is the mitral valve, between the left atrium and ventricle. Drag the pressure difference across it:

With the atrium even 2 or 3 mmHg higher, the leaflets swing open and blood pours through. Once the ventricle's pressure climbs past the atrium's, the same blood pushes them shut. Unheld, they would blow through like an umbrella turning inside out; the chordae tendineae, cords tied to the papillary muscles, stop them just as they meet.

Now on one leaflet. It flips back, and every squeeze sends a jet the wrong way. A ruptured cord, one complication of a heart attack, can flood the lungs this way.

Heart valves don't open because the heart tells them to. They are passive flaps; the pressure on either side decides everything. That is why a valve's timing tells you so much about the pressures around it.

One heartbeat, slowed down

Every beat runs through the same sequence, and the valves split it into parts. Here is the heart cut open again with a clock and the volume of the left ventricle. A resting beat lasts 0.8 seconds. Move into it and watch which valves are open:

The atria contract first, adding the last fifth or so to ventricles that are already mostly full. Then the ventricles squeeze, and for about 50 milliseconds all four valves are shut: the inlets because the ventricle's pressure is now above the atrium's, the outlets because it is still below the artery's. The ventricle squeezes a sealed bag, and its pressure climbs from about 10 to 80 mmHg with no change in volume. This is isovolumic contraction.

When the pressure passes the aorta's, the aortic valve opens and blood leaves for about 0.28 seconds: , 70 of the 123 mL. Then the muscle relaxes with every valve shut until its pressure drops below the atrium's, the mitral valve opens, and the ventricle refills, fast at first, as the relaxing wall almost sucks blood inThe right side runs the same sequence at about a fifth of the pressure, its valves moving a few hundredths of a second out of step with the left's..

Draw the pressures on one time axis and the whole beat fits on one chart, a Wiggers diagramNamed after the American physiologist Carl Wiggers, who recorded these pressures in the early twentieth century.. First, a question about the moment the aortic valve shuts.

At the instant the aortic valve snaps shut, which pressure is higher: the ventricle's or the aorta's?

The ventricle starts relaxing while the blood is still coasting forward. Its pressure falls below the aorta's for a few hundredths of a second, the flow reverses, and the backflow catches the cusps and slams them shut. That reversal leaves a notch in the aortic pressure.

Every event on the chart is a crossing of two curves. The ventricle rising through the atrium shuts the mitral valve, and rising through the aorta opens the aortic valve; falling back reverses them. The atrium's pressure has two humps: its own squeeze, then a rise while the mitral valve is shut and the lungs keep pouring blood in.

The aorta's pressure only sags from about 120 to 80 mmHg between beats, with a small notch where the valve caught the backflow. And the ECG at the bottom runs ahead of the mechanics: the QRS comes a few hundredths of a second before the ventricle's pressure starts to rise.

Why the pressure never drops to zero

The ventricle pushes for only a third of each beat, yet blood flows through your organs all the time. The reason is the aorta, an elastic tube thick with stretchy elastin. During ejection it balloons out and stores about half of the stroke volume, and between beats it recoils and pushes it onEngineers call this a windkessel, German for "air chamber", after the air-filled domes on old fire pumps that smoothed the strokes into a steady jet .. A beat as a loop

Plot the ventricle's pressure against its volume instead of against time, and the beat becomes a loop it goes around once per beat, counterclockwise. Watch the dot:

Along the bottom the ventricle fills at low pressure; up the right side it squeezes sealed; across the top it ejects; down the left side it relaxes, sealed again. The loop's width is the stroke volume, and divided by the volume at the start of the squeeze it is the ejection fraction, the number on most heart scan reports: 57% here, and usually between about 55% and 75% in healthy hearts . : its area is the work done on the blood each beat, about one joule.

Three different things change the loop. Preload is how much the ventricle is filled before it squeezes. Afterload is what it pushes against, mostly the resistance of the small arteries. Here e(t) runs from 0 to 1 and back, with a shape fitted to measured human hearts (a "double Hill" curve, scaled so that systole shortens less than diastole as the rate rises) . The second term is the relaxed wall, which stiffens steeply as it fills. Blood leaves through an aortic valve that carries the inertia of the moving blood, so the flow reverses briefly before the valve shuts, and enters a three-element windkessel: a short stiff segment, an elastic reservoir of compliance \brass{C}, and the resistance of the small arteries R . The left atrium is a smaller elastic chamber filled from the lungs that stiffens for 0.1 s when it contracts.

The page integrates these equations in steps of 0.2 milliseconds for ten beats and keeps the last one. With \red{E_{max}} = 2.6 mmHg/mL, R = 1.1 mmHg·s/mL and \brass{C} = 1.4 mL/mmHg it gives 118/78 mmHg, 123 mL filled, 70 mL ejected, and 5.3 L/min at 75 beats a minute. The model has no right heart, no reflected pressure waves, and no nerves: everything the body adjusts by itself is a slider here.

The spark that times each beat

A heart lifted out of the body keeps beating for a while with no nerves attached. Its rhythm comes from a few thousand cells in the right atrium's wall, the sinus node. They never rest at a steady voltage: after each beat, positive ions leak in until the voltage creeps up to a threshold, the cell fires, and the creep starts again. Turn up adrenaline or the vagus nerve and watch the slope of the creep:

With the node fires about 100 times a minute in a young adultMeasured by blocking both sets of nerves with drugs; the rate falls with age .. At rest the vagus nerve brakes it to 60 or 70; adrenaline steepens the creep and can push it past 180. The sinus node only sets the tempo.

Beneath it, a ventricular cell's action potential has a long flat plateau, about a quarter of a second, while calcium flows in and the cell contracts. Until it ends, the cell can't fire again, so heart muscle can't be held in a cramp: every squeeze is followed by a forced relaxation long enough to refill.

From the sinus node the wave must reach billions of muscle cells in the right order: the atria, a deliberate pause, then the ventricles from the inside out and from the tip up. Here it is on a section of the heart, slowed ten times. Move through the first :

The wave crosses both atria in about a tenth of a secondA fast band, Bachmann's bundle, carries it across to the left atrium.. Then it hits a ring of fibrous tissue that doesn't conduct at all. The only way through is the AV node, which is deliberately slow, about 5 centimeters a second . The wave waits there for while the atria finish emptying.

Past the node it runs fast down the His bundle in the septum and through the Purkinje fibers lining both ventricles, at 2 to 4 meters a second, so the ventricles light from the inside of the tip and the wave crawls outward through the wall. In isolated human hearts the inner left ventricle lit at several spots within milliseconds, and the base near the valves came last . That order squeezes blood up toward the exits, like toothpaste from the bottom of the tube.

Reading the wave from the skin

An ECG picks up this wave from electrodes on the arms and legs. At any instant, the wavefront between fired cells and resting ones carries current, like a battery. Add up every little battery and you get one arrow, the heart vector, which swings as the wave moves; a pair of electrodes reads how much it points along the line between them. The small first bump, the P wave, is the atria. The flat stretch is the wave , too few cells to register. The tall QRS complex is the ventricles, the arrow pointing down and left toward the thick left ventricle. During the every cell is charged, there is no boundary, and the trace sits flat: the ST segment. Remember that flat line; it is where a heart attack shows itself.

Then the T wave, as the ventricles recover. It points the same way as the QRS because the cells activated last recover first: recovery runs back the other way, and two reversals cancel.

Keeping up with a sprint

A fit young adult running flat out needs three or four times the resting 5 liters a minute. Every lever pulls at once: adrenaline speeds the sinus node and strengthens the squeeze, working leg muscles pump blood back so the heart fills harder, and the muscles' arterioles open wide. The heart's own blood supply

The heart is half a percent of body weight but uses about a tenth of its oxygen at rest, and it already pulls about three quarters of the oxygen out of the blood passing through it, so the only way to get more is more flow . The wall is far too thick to be fed from the blood inside. It has its own arteries, the coronary arteries, which leave the aorta just above the aortic valve and run over its surface like a crown:

The left anterior descending artery feeds the front of the left ventricle and most of the septum, more muscle than any other. The circumflex wraps round the left side, and the right coronary artery round the right and the back. Each is 3 to 4 mm wide, and together they carry about 225 mL a minute at rest, 4 to 5% of the output .

There is a catch. When the ventricle squeezes, the pressure inside its wall is as high as in the arteries feeding it, highest in the inner layers, and the squeeze pinches the branches shut. So the left ventricle is fed mainly between beats. Speed up the heart, :

At rest the heart is relaxed nearly 60% of each beat, and about three quarters of the flow arrives then. At each squeeze is a third shorter, but the gaps shrink to under a third of their resting length. The heart works hardest exactly when its feeding window is shortest. A healthy artery copes by widening its small branches, raising flow four to five times . A narrowed one can't.

How an artery clogs

Atherosclerosis starts decades before anyone notices. Where an artery's lining is irritated, cholesterol-carrying particles slip into the wall and get stuck. White blood cells move in to clear them, gorge on the fat, and die there, and the debris collects into a soft core under a fibrous cap of scar tissue . This is stable angina: chest pain on exertion, when the muscle asks for more than the artery can bring, fading within minutes of rest. A warning, not yet damage.

Most heart attacks don't come from the tightest narrowing slowly closing. They come from a plaque that breaks. Only the fibrous cap separates the blood from the fatty core, and inflammation can thin it from beneath; caps that ruptured were mostly under 65 micrometers, thinner than a hair . Platelets stick within seconds, fibrin knits them together, and the clot grows until it seals the channel. A plaque that narrowed the artery by a third has blocked it in minutes.

In men who died suddenly with a fresh coronary clot, about two thirds of the clots sat on a ruptured plaque and the rest on a plaque whose surface had worn away . makes platelets less sticky, so the clot grows more slowly and may not close the artery. A heart attack, minute by minute

When a clot seals a coronary artery, the muscle downstream stops squeezing within seconds and burns through its oxygen within a minute or so. For about the first 20 minutes the damage is reversible. After that cells start to die, and the death spreads as a wavefront, which Reimer and Jennings mapped by tying off coronary arteries in dogs for different times . The inner layer dies first: it is squeezed hardest, sits at the end of the supply, and relies most on flow between beats. From there death advances outward and sideways over hours. At a little over a third of the muscle at risk was dead; at , more than half; by a day, most of it. Cardiologists say it in three words: time is muscle.

The ECG sees this in the first minutes. Injured cells can't hold their resting voltage, so current leaks between them and healthy muscle, and the flat ST segment lifts off the baseline. ST elevation of at least 1 mm in two neighboring leads, a little more in some chest leads, marks a STEMI: the artery is fully closed and every minute counts . Over the dead muscle falls silent, the R wave shrinks (in a real heart often leaving a deep Q wave), and the T wave inverts. Weeks later the ST segment is flat again, but the lost R wave stays.

Opening the artery

So open the artery as early as possible. Clot-dissolving drugs, given into a vein, switch on the body's own enzyme for breaking up fibrin. Or a cardiologist threads a wire from the wrist or groin into the blocked artery and pushes the clot and plaque aside with a balloonAndreas Grüntzig first did this in a coronary artery in 1977 . What to take away

The heart is two pumps kept in step by their own filling, with valves that obey pressure and a wave that pauses at the AV node, then lights the ventricles from the inside out. It feeds itself between beats through arteries that can collect plaque for decades unseen. Most heart attacks come when a thin cap tears and a clot seals the channel; then the muscle dies in a wave, and how much is saved depends on how fast the artery is opened.

So if you or someone near you has chest pain or pressure lasting more than a few minutes, especially with breathlessness, sweating, nausea, or pain spreading to the arm, jaw, or back, call your local emergency number at once. Don't drive yourself, and don't wait to see if it passes.

This page is for learning, not medical advice. Its models are deliberately simplified, and several numbers come from dog experiments. For questions about your own heart, ask a doctor.
