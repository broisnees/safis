How a Cell Builds a Protein

How a cell copies one gene out of its DNA into a message, reads that message three letters at a time, and strings amino acids into the protein the gene describes, and what a single changed letter can do.

  • A gene is a stretch of DNA written in four letters. Because A pairs only with T and G only with C, either strand can rebuild the other.
  • To use a gene, RNA polymerase copies one strand into a messenger RNA, and in our cells the parts that don't code are cut out before the message leaves the nucleus.
  • A ribosome reads the message three letters at a time. Adapter molecules called tRNAs match each three-letter word and bring the amino acid it stands for, and the ribosome links them into a chain.
  • The machinery is fast and makes about one mistake per 10,000 amino acids. A changed letter in the gene can do nothing, swap one amino acid, end the chain early, or garble everything after it.
  • The finished chain folds by itself into a working shape. In sickle cell disease one swapped amino acid on that surface is enough to bend whole red blood cells out of shape.

Every red blood cell in your body is packed with about 270 million molecules of hemoglobin, the protein that picks up oxygen in your lungs and drops it off everywhere else. Your bone marrow makes more than two million new red cells every second, so it is building hemoglobin at a staggering rate. The instructions for it are written in your DNA.

Each hemoglobin molecule is four chains of smaller units called amino acids: two alpha chains and two beta chains. The beta chain is 146 amino acids long, and its recipe is a stretch of DNA on chromosome 11 called the HBB gene. This page follows that one gene, letter by letter, from the DNA to a finished, folded protein. Every sequence you'll see is the real human one.

Three molecules carry the instructions in turn. The DNA holds the permanent copy and never leaves the nucleus. A working copy of one gene, the messenger RNA or mRNA, carries it out to the rest of the cell. There a machine called the ribosome reads the message and strings amino acids together in the order it spells, and the chain folds up into a protein. Biologists call this one-way flow, from DNA to RNA to protein, the central dogma.

Each step raises a question. How can a string of four chemical letters describe a chain of twenty kinds of amino acid? How does the cell find one gene among twenty thousand? And how does a molecule with no eyes read a message? The answers start with the shape of DNA.

Four letters

DNA is two long strands wound around each other. Each strand has a backbone of sugar and phosphate, and hanging off every sugar is one of four bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G), and cytosine (C). The bases of the two strands meet in the middle, in pairs. Here are the first 21 letters of the HBB gene's code. Drag to turn it, and use the slider to take the twist out:

Twisted up, the molecule is the famous double helix, 2 nanometers across, with a full turn every 10.5 base pairs. Take the twist out and it turns into a ladder: the backbones are the rails and the base pairs are the rungs. The twist matters for how DNA packs and how proteins grip it, but the information is all in the rungs. Notice that every rung is the same length, and that green always meets red, and orange always meets blue.

That rule comes from the shapes of the bases. A and G are big, with two rings of atoms; T and C are small, with one. Along the edge that faces the partner, each base carries hydrogen atoms that can be shared (donors) and atoms that can accept them (acceptors). Choose a base for each strand and see which pairs fit between the two backbones:

Two big bases overlap; two small ones leave a gap. Only a big base with a small one spans the space between the backbones, and of those, only two combinations line up a donor against an acceptor at every point. A with T makes two hydrogen bonds, and G with C makes three. A with C, or G with T, have the right size but put like against like: an H against an H, a dot against a dot.

A pairs with T and G pairs with C. So each strand of DNA fixes the order of letters on the other.

That one rule is what lets DNA be copied. Pull the two strands apart, and each loose base can only accept its own partner from the cell's pool of free bases. Slide the fork along to unzip this piece of the gene and let new bases move in:

Behind the fork the single double strand has become two, and they spell the same thing. Each copy keeps one old strand, drawn with a solid backbone, and gains one new one, because the old strand served as the pattern, or template. In a real cell, an enzyme called DNA polymerase does the fetching and joining, about a thousand letters a second in bacteria and a few dozen in our own cells, from many forks at once.Our cells copy all 3 billion base pairs in about eight hours by starting at tens of thousands of places along the chromosomes at the same time.

The same pairing rule runs through everything that follows. A cell uses it to copy its genes when it divides, to make working copies of single genes, and, in the end, to read them.

Genes

Your cells carry about 3.1 billion base pairs in each set of chromosomes. Printed at one letter per millimeter, they would stretch about 3,100 kilometers. Somewhere in there is the beta-globin recipe. Drag the zoom slider from the whole of chromosome 11 down to single letters, and watch for the gene:

Out at the scale of the chromosome, the gene is invisible: chromosome 11 alone holds 135 million base pairs, and HBB is about 1,600 of them, near one tip. Closer in, it turns out to have neighbors. The two HBG genes make the globin chains a fetus uses, HBE1 one used by the early embryo, and HBD a minor adult chain. HBBP1 is a broken copy that makes nothing. Closer still, the gene itself has parts. The thick blocks are the stretches that end up in the message, called exons, and the thin lines between them are introns, which are copied and then thrown away. The thickest parts are the ones that spell out amino acids.

Look at the two strands at the bottom of the zoom. The start of the code sits 50 letters past the place where copying begins, and copying begins just after a short stretch marked promoter. A gene, then, is more than its code. It is a region with a landing site for the copying machinery, a stretch to copy, and signals along the way that say where to cut and where to stop.

The cell finds that landing site with proteins that grip particular short sequences. In many genes, this one included, the first to arrive is the TATA-binding protein, which grips a sequence close to TATAAAA about 30 letters before the start. Slide it along the beta-globin promoter and watch how many of its seven letters match:

Most places match two or three letters, which is what random DNA does, and the protein slides on. At position the letters read CATAAAA, six of seven, and it grips. Gripping bends the DNA sharply, and other proteins gather on the bend, including RNA polymerase, the machine that will copy the gene. They set it down about 30 letters further on, facing the gene. Strands of DNA have a direction, written 5′ to 3′ after the way the sugars point, and the promoter's orientation decides which way the polymerase runs and therefore which strand it reads.The beta-globin promoter has more such boxes further upstream, CCAAT and CACCC. Single-letter changes in the CACCC and TATA-like boxes turn up in people with mild beta-thalassemia, in whom the gene is read less often than normal.

Real cells add a great deal on top of this: enhancer sequences thousands of letters away, proteins that switch the gene on only in developing red cells, and DNA packed so tightly that a gene has to be unwound before anything can land on it. The core idea survives all of that. A short sequence near the gene says "start here, go this way."

Copying the gene

RNA polymerase now does what the copying fork did, on a small scale. It pries open a short stretch of the double helix, reads one strand, and builds a matching strand of RNA, a close chemical cousin of DNA. RNA uses the base uracil (U) where DNA has T; it pairs with A just the same. Drag the slider to let the polymerase make the first letters of the beta-globin message:

Inside the polymerase about 14 letters of DNA are held open as a bubble. Only the bottom, template strand is read. Each new RNA letter is the partner of the template letter across from it, so the RNA comes out with the same sequence as the top strand, U for T. That top strand is called the coding strand for exactly that reason. The newest eight letters of RNA stay paired to the template, and then the RNA peels away and the DNA closes up behind the polymerase.

The first 50 letters aren't code at all. The message carries a lead-in, and only at letter 51 does AUG appear, where the ribosome will later start reading.

How long does this take? Measured in living human cells, RNA polymerase moves at roughly 2 to 4 thousand letters a minute. That makes beta-globin quick. Choose a speed and compare it with two much longer genes:

At 3 thousand letters a minute, about 50 a second, beta-globin's 1,608 letters take about half a minute. The gene for titin, the largest protein in muscle, takes over an hour and a half. Dystrophin, a muscle protein whose gene stretches over 2.2 million letters, takes more than half a day: a single copy, started in the morning, is finished in the evening. Measurements in cultured muscle cells put it at about 16 hours . Most of those letters are introns, which brings us to what happens to the copy.

Cutting out the introns

In bacteria the RNA is ready to read the moment it's made. In our cells the first copy, the pre-mRNA, still contains the introns, and a large machine called the spliceosome cuts them out and joins the exons end to end. Each intron starts with GU and ends with AG, part of how the spliceosome recognizes it. Drag the slider to splice the beta-globin copy:

At the start, more than half of the copy is intron; when both are gone, the 1,608-letter copy has become a message of 628 letters, and the darker coding part, 444 letters, now runs without a break. Beta-globin is a small, tidy gene. A typical human gene has around ten exons separated by introns that are thousands of letters long, so the cut-out parts usually outweigh the message many times over.

Splicing has a payoff. Many genes can be spliced in more than one way, skipping or keeping particular exons, so one gene can make several related proteins. The message also gets a chemical cap on its front end and a tail of a couple of hundred A's on its back end, which protect it and help it get read. Then it is exported through a pore in the nuclear envelope, and the cell faces its real puzzle: turning four letters into twenty amino acids.

The genetic code

Proteins are built from twenty kinds of amino acid, and the message has only four letters. One letter per amino acid can't work, so the cell must read words. Step up the word length and count how many different words there are:

Two letters give 16 words, four short. Three letters give 64, more than enough, and that is what cells use: the message is read in non-overlapping three-letter words called codons. With 64 codons for 20 amino acids there are spares, and the spares aren't wasted. Most amino acids have several codons, and three codons mean stop.

Eight of the sixteen boxes are a single band: for , , or any CU codon, the answer is leucine, whatever the third letter. In most other boxes the third letter only splits the box in two. That is why the code is called redundant, and why so many changes in the third letter of a codon change nothing in the protein. Only (methionine) and (tryptophan) stand alone.

The table has other order in it. Codons with U in the middle all code for oily amino acids, so a change in the first or third letter there usually swaps one oily amino acid for another. And AUG has a second job: it is where reading starts. The three dark codons, UAA, UAG, and UGA, are where it stops.The table was worked out between 1961 and 1966, starting when Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei fed a string of nothing but U's to a cell extract and got back a chain of nothing but phenylalanine.

Each message has three possible reading frames, and only one is the protein.

The ribosome finds the right one by starting at the front of the message and sliding along until it meets the first AUG. In beta-globin there is no AUG in the 50-letter lead-in, so the first one is the real start.

Adapters

A codon can't grab an amino acid by itself: there's nothing about the shape of GAG that fits glutamate. The link is made by a family of small RNA molecules called transfer RNAs, or tRNAs. Each is about 76 letters long and folds back on itself into four short double-stranded arms. Drag to turn this one, and use the slider to fold its arms into place:

On paper a tRNA is drawn as a flat cloverleaf. Folded, its arms stack end to end into an L. At the tip of one arm is the anticodon, three unpaired letters that can pair with a codon. At the tip of the other, about 7 nanometers away, the amino acid is attached. Nothing in the tRNA touches both at once. The connection between a codon and its amino acid is made earlier, by a set of enzymes, one for each amino acid, that recognize their own tRNAs and load each with the right amino acid.These loading enzymes, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, check their work: several of them have a second pocket that clips off an amino acid that was loaded by mistake. So the code really lives in those enzymes.

The anticodon pairs with the codon the same way DNA strands pair, running in the opposite direction. Choose a codon and offer it different tRNAs:

A pairs all three letters the way DNA does. But a tRNA with the anticodon UUC also accepts GAG: the first two letters pair normally, and at the third a G sits across from a U, a looser fit that still holds. Francis Crick predicted this sloppiness at the third position in 1966 and called it wobble . Some tRNAs carry a modified base, inosine, that wobbles with three different letters, so one tRNA can , CUC, and CUA.

Wobble explains the shape of the codon table. The third letter matters least because it is the one checked least strictly, and a cell gets by with fewer kinds of tRNA than there are codons. The first two letters, though, must pair perfectly, and that is where the ribosome comes in.

The ribosome

The ribosome is where codon meets tRNA. It is built from two parts, a small subunit and a large one, each a tangle of RNA and dozens of proteins, and the message runs through the gap between them. Inside are three slots for tRNAs, side by side along the message, named A, P, and E. Turn the model, pull the subunits apart, and point at the parts to name them:

The message enters on the right and is read moving left to right, 5′ to 3′. The A site is where a new tRNA arrives with its amino acid, the P site holds the tRNA carrying the chain built so far, and the E site holds an emptied tRNA on its way out. The chain leaves through a tunnel in the large subunit about 30 to 40 amino acids long, so the first part of a protein is already out in the cell while the rest is being made.

Step through three rounds of reading at the start of the beta-globin message. Watch where the chain is attached at each step:

Each round has the same four steps. A tRNA whose anticodon pairs with the codon in the A site settles in; the wrong ones are turned away. The large subunit then moves the whole chain off the tRNA in the P site and joins it to the amino acid on the new tRNA, making a peptide bond. Next the ribosome shifts exactly three letters along the message, which moves the tRNAs from A to P and from P to E, and leaves the A site empty over the next codon. The empty tRNA drops out of the E site to be reloaded.

The peptide bond itself is made by RNA. When the structure of the large subunit was solved at atomic detail in 2000, there was no protein anywhere near the spot where bonds form, only ribosomal RNA . Reading ends when a stop codon reaches the A site. No tRNA matches it; a protein called a release factor fits there instead and cuts the finished chain loose.

A ribosome doesn't need the message to itself. As soon as one has moved off the start, another can begin. Set how often a new ribosome starts on the beta-globin message and let it run:

Each ribosome takes about 26 seconds to make one beta-globin chain at 5.6 codons a second , and its chain grows longer as it moves toward the stop. With a new ribosome , eight or nine read the message at once and a finished chain comes off every 3 seconds. Start them and they jam: a ribosome covers about 30 letters, so they queue nose to tail and the output is set by how fast they can clear the start. A message being read by many ribosomes at once is called a polysome, and it is one reason a single gene copy can turn out so much protein.
